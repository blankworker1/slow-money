# No Names, No Numbers #2: The Joker's Tale

*A story from the cells.*

## I. The Box

On evenings when the rain kept people late, and the bread was not yet gone, the old man would reach under the bench and bring out a wooden box, dark with handling, and set it on the table among the cups.

The first time she saw it, she thought it was a game. It was, and it wasn't.

Inside lay a deck of cards, soft at the edges, and a die, and a coin. The cards were of an old pattern: plain cream faces with red and black pips, and court cards painted in stiff, bright robes, kings with orbs and swords, a queen holding a flower, knaves leaning on their shields. There were no figures in the corners. You could see the pips, of course. Nobody counted them.

"Only the suit," the old man said, laying cards out before her one by one. "Hearts are what people feel for one another. Diamonds are places and things. Clubs are struggle. Spades are secrets, and danger." He said it once, slowly. She never heard anyone say it again; after that evening, it was simply known.

She picked up a knave. Beside his feet, in faded letters, he had a name. She opened her mouth.

"No, thank you," said the baker, without looking up, and the girl who was the Rememberer laughed into her soup. The knave went back into the deck, still nameless.

The die was carved, not printed. Where she expected pips there were pictures, burned into the wood: a crown, a star, a key, a candle, a boat, a house. Turning it in her fingers, she found that the crown always lay opposite the house, the star opposite the boat, the key opposite the candle, as if whoever made it had wanted every tale to have its other side.

The coin was the strangest thing in the box. It was gold, or something like it, worn smooth by hands, with no number on it and no words. On one face was a letter B, crossed by strange upright lines. On the other was a pattern of fine traces and small dots, like the plan of a city seen from very high up.

"What is it?" she asked.

The old man turned it over. "The mark," he said, showing her the letter. "That's fortune." He turned it back. "The maze. That's trouble."

"But what was it? Before?"

He thought about it with real care, and then shook his head. "I don't know. Nobody I asked knew either. It was given to me with the box, and it'll go with the box when I'm gone." He weighed it in his palm. "It must have meant something once. Things like this always did."

The baker dealt. She did not count. She took a pinch from the deck by feel, as much as felt like an evening, slid the Joker into it, and squared the little pile in the middle of the table. The rest of the deck went back in the box.

"How long will it last?" the newcomer asked, before she could stop herself.

This time several voices said "No, thank you" at once, warmly, and she found she was laughing too.

That first evening the bread ran out before the Joker came. Nobody minded. The pile of laid cards was gathered up in its order and wrapped in a cloth, and the tale was left exactly where it stood: a woman on a boat, in fog, hearing someone call from the shore. Next time, the one who mended the chair unwrapped the cloth, turned over the last card, and picked the tale up from the fog as if no time had passed at all.

It was the baker who told her, walking her home, what the game was for.

"The girl," she said. "The Rememberer. Do you know how she was chosen?"

She didn't.

"Nobody chose her. Nobody voted. She drew the Joker one night when she was barely more than a child, and she told the whole tale back without a single slip, and the table went quiet the way it does. After that, whenever she drew it, the room went quiet. And when the old Rememberer couldn't carry the law any more, everyone already knew who would." The baker smiled in the dark. "That's all it is. A game for learning to carry things."

## II. The Joker

The die came down on the key. The coin showed the maze. So it would be a tale of a secret, and the hero would be a stranger.

The woman who made the beans drew the first card, a spade, and the coin showed the maze again. "A woman comes to a town where everyone wears a mask," she said. "She doesn't know why. And on her first night, someone leaves a loaf of bread on her doorstep, with no name on it."

The one who brought the apricots felt the table glance at her and laughed with everyone else.

It went round. The one who mended the chair drew a diamond and gave the stranger a map with streets missing. The baker drew a heart, the coin showed the mark, and the stranger found a friend in the woman who sold the bread. The young man who looked properly drew a club and set a guard on the town gate who asked everyone their number. The girl who was the Rememberer drew a spade, and the stranger learned that the bread had been left by someone who had died long ago.

Round it went, and the pile grew, and the tale grew stranger and better. Then the one who brought the apricots turned over the Joker.

The table made a soft sound, part delight and part sympathy. She picked up the pile with both hands, and held it, and looked at the top card, and her mind was perfectly empty.

"A woman comes to a town," someone murmured.

"A woman comes to a town where everyone wears a mask," she said, and the rest began to come. The loaf on the doorstep. The map with streets missing. The friend who sold bread. She turned the cards one by one, and where she lost her way, a word came from somewhere round the table: "the guard", "the gate", "the one who died". Nobody told it for her. They only held out a hand when she stumbled.

She came to the end of the pile. The kitchen was quiet. She drew the last card, a heart, and tossed the coin, and it came down on the mark.

"And when the stranger finally found the house where the bread had come from," she said slowly, "there was no one there. Only a table, laid a little wider than it needed to be. So she sat down, and waited, and in time others came in out of the cold. And she understood that the loaf had never been left by one person. It had been left by everyone who would ever sit there. Including her."

For a moment nobody said anything. Then the old man reached for the last of the bread and broke it, and the evening came to its end.

In the hallway, putting on her persona, she found the girl who was the Rememberer watching her, thoughtfully, the way you look at someone you will need to remember.
