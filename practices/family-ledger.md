# The Family Ledger

The Family Ledger is a practice for deciding what a family records and what it leaves unrecorded. It does not ask families to keep accounts of each other. It asks them to sort their exchanges into three places, so that nothing important is left in the fourth: the hidden ledger nobody speaks about.

## Why a ledger, or not

Much of family life runs without anyone keeping score. Meals are cooked, children are collected, the sick are cared for, and nobody writes it down. This is not a gap in the family's accounts. It is the family working as it should, and turning it into debts would damage it.

But every family also keeps a hidden ledger. A loan never repaid. A sibling who cared for a parent for years while another lived abroad. A promise about the house. These accounts are never written, rarely spoken, and they surface at the worst moments: at a wedding, a divorce, a funeral, a will.

The Family Ledger brings the hidden ledger into the open so that each item can go where it belongs.

## The three places

### The table: given freely, never recorded

Care, time, meals, lifts, childcare, help with a move, a bed for the night. What is given at the table is a gift, and a gift is not a debt. Nothing on the table is written down, and nobody may later claim it as something owed.

### The slate: spoken aloud, settled, then wiped

Some exchanges between family members are real obligations and are better treated as such: a loan between siblings, a shared cost, money advanced against an inheritance, an agreement about who pays for what. These go on the slate.

The slate is visible to everyone concerned and temporary by design. Each item says what is owed, between whom, and what will settle it. When it is settled, it is wiped clean. A slate can be a literal chalkboard in the kitchen, a page in the family notebook, or a shared note, as long as everyone concerned can see it and nothing stays on it forever.

### The chronicle: decisions and reasons, kept for good

The chronicle is the family's permanent record. It does not record sums. It records what the family decided and why:

- *We kept the land rather than selling it, because we want it to stay in use by the family.*
- *We agreed that whoever cares for our parents will live in the house.*
- *We paid off the car loan early, because debt makes your grandfather anxious and we would rather he slept.*

Each entry has a date and the names of those present, and nothing else. No figures, no record of who argued for what, no grievances. Over time the chronicle becomes a memory of how the family thought about money, and it is handed down with whatever money there is.

## Running the practice

The Family Ledger is best held at a [Kitchen Table](kitchen-table.md) after the family has already had at least one first conversation. Read the ground rules aloud before you begin.

### Step one: name the table

Go around the table. Each person names something they have been given by the family that should never be counted as a debt. Say these aloud. Do not write them down. This step comes first on purpose: it reminds everyone what the family already does well.

### Step two: open the hidden ledger

Ask gently: *Is there anything someone feels is owed, or feels they owe?*

There may be silence. That is fine. Nobody has to speak, and a family may need several Kitchen Tables before anything is said. If an item is raised, listen without arguing about it. Then decide together where it goes:

- **Release it.** The person who is owed says aloud that it is now a gift. Once released, it is never raised again. It has moved to the table.
- **Put it on the slate.** It is a real obligation. Write down what is owed, between whom, and what will settle it.
- **Record a decision.** Sometimes the item is really a question about the future, such as who will live in the house. The family decides, and the decision goes in the chronicle.

An item that cannot be sorted today can wait. Say so, and agree when to return to it.

### Step three: write the chronicle

One person writes the chronicle entry by hand: the date, who was present, and any decisions made with their reasons. Read it aloud before closing the book, so everyone agrees it says what was decided.

## Who keeps the chronicle

The chronicle is best kept as a bound book, written by hand, and stored in the family home. One person acts as its keeper, and the role can pass between generations at turning points: a wedding, a move, a death, a child coming of age. Handing over the chronicle is itself worth marking at a Kitchen Table.

## Inheritance

Inheritance is where the hidden ledger does the most harm, and it is the reason this practice matters most. Families that talk before a death find it far easier than families that talk after.

The chronicle can record wishes and the reasons behind them, so that nobody is surprised and nobody has to guess what a parent intended. But the chronicle is not a will and has no legal force. Wishes about property and money should also be put in a proper will, with advice from a lawyer or notary.

## When not to use this practice

The Family Ledger is for families who can talk with care. Where money is part of control or abuse within a family, or where old conflicts are too raw, this practice may make things worse. In those cases, outside support should come first.
