# The Heirloom

The Heirloom is a practice for deciding, on purpose, what a family passes on. It closes the cycle of the Slow Money practices: after remembering, recording and seeing the family's economy clearly, the family chooses what to carry forward and what to let end.

The manifesto describes financial mindset as an heirloom: something made carefully, kept in use and handed on on purpose. This practice treats that literally.

## Three kinds of heirloom

Every family passes on three things, whether it chooses to or not.

- **Things:** money, land, a house, tools, objects. The kind of inheritance everyone recognises.
- **Knowledge:** skills, stories, recipes, trades, the memories gathered in [Money Memories](money-memories.md).
- **Mindset:** habits and attitudes to money. Whether it is spoken about or hidden, spent at once or kept for later. How far ahead the family looks.

Things can be lost in a single generation. Knowledge and mindset, once handed on, can outlast them. Most families plan carefully for the first kind and leave the other two to chance. This practice gives all three the same care.

## Running the practice

Hold the Heirloom at a [Kitchen Table](kitchen-table.md), ideally after the family has already tried at least one of the other practices. Read the ground rules aloud.

### Step one: what we received

Each person names one heirloom they received from an older generation: one thing, one piece of knowledge, or one habit of mind. The oldest person speaks first.

The heirloom does not have to be good. Some of what we inherit, such as a fear of spending or a habit of hiding money, we would rather not pass on.

### Step two: what we keep

Together, choose up to three heirlooms the family wants to carry forward, at least one of them knowledge or mindset rather than a thing. For each, ask: *How will we keep it in use?* An heirloom locked in a drawer is only a keepsake. A skill must be practised, a story retold, a habit lived.

### Step three: what ends with us

Now choose one thing the family does not want to pass on. A silence, a fear, a pattern of debt, a way of treating money as a secret.

Say it aloud. The family agrees that this ends here. Naming what ends is often the most powerful part of the practice, because inherited habits run silently until someone decides to stop them.

### Step four: the chronicle

Write the entry in the family chronicle (see [The Family Ledger](family-ledger.md)): the date, who was present, the heirlooms the family chose to keep, and the one it chose to end, each with a short reason.

## The Heirloom Letter

Any member of the family can write a letter about money to a future generation: a child, a grandchild, or someone not yet born. It says what the writer learned, what they hope for, and what they wish they had known.

The letter is sealed and kept with the chronicle, marked with when it should be opened: at eighteen, at a first job, at a wedding, or at a date many years ahead. Some families keep a physical object with it, such as a coin, a savings book or a tool, as a reminder that the letter belongs to the family's economy as well as its memory.

A letter opened twenty years after it was written is one of the few things that can carry a long view across a generation intact.

## Handing on the chronicle

At some point the chronicle itself will pass to a new keeper. This is a good moment for a Heirloom Kitchen Table. The current keeper reads a few entries aloud, the family adds a new one, and the book changes hands.

## Inheritance

Talking about heirlooms will lead many families towards inheritance of things: money, property, the family home. The chronicle can record wishes and the reasons behind them, so that nobody has to guess later. But it has no legal force. Wishes about property and money should also be set out in a proper will, with advice from a lawyer or notary.

## Back to the table

The Heirloom completes the cycle, but the cycle does not end. The heirlooms chosen here become next year's memories, and the next Kitchen Table begins again with what the family received.
