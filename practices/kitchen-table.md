# The Kitchen Table

The Kitchen Table is a family conversation about money, held around a shared meal, with every generation present. It is the first practice in the Slow Money series and the one all the others return to.

The aim of the first conversation is not to fix anything. It is to make the family's economy visible, and to hear how each generation sees it.

## What you need

- A table and a meal. Money is easier to talk about with food in front of you, and the weekly shop is already something the family shares.
- As many generations as you can gather. Two is good; three is better. A grandparent on a phone or video call counts.
- About an hour. Less for young children.
- Optional: a notebook that stays with the family. This becomes the family chronicle (see [The Family Ledger](family-ledger.md)).

Nothing else. No bank statements, no spreadsheets, no figures.

## Before you begin

Anyone can call a Kitchen Table, including a teenager. Tell everyone in advance that the meal will include a conversation about money, and that nobody will be asked to share numbers.

At the start, read the ground rules aloud. They are short:

1. Nobody has to share numbers.
2. Gifts stay gifts.
3. Every age speaks. The youngest may ask anything; the oldest speaks first about the past.
4. What is said here stays here.
5. We stop when it stops being kind.

## The first conversation

The first Kitchen Table moves through three rounds: the past, the present and the future. Go around the table in each round. Anyone may pass.

### Round one: memories

The oldest person answers first. Choose one or two questions, not all of them.

- What did money mean in your home when you were a child?
- What was the first money you ever earned, and what did you do with it?
- What did your parents never talk about when it came to money?
- Was there a time when money was very short? What did the family do?
- What did your family trust to keep its savings safe?

### Round two: habits

Now the question turns to this household. Anyone may answer first.

- What do we do with money in this house that we have never talked about?
- Who decides how money is spent here? Who knows the most about it?
- When money comes into this house, where does it go first?
- What is one money habit you think you learned from someone at this table?

Children can take part in this round with simpler questions:

- Where do you think our money comes from?
- What do we spend the most money on?
- What would you do if someone gave you some money today?

### Round three: hopes

The youngest person answers first.

- What is one thing this family does with money that you want to keep?
- What is one thing you would like us to do differently?
- What do you hope this family will still have in thirty years?

## Closing

End by going around the table once more. Each person names one thing they heard that surprised them.

If the family keeps a notebook, one person writes a single line: the date, who was present, and one thing the family decided or wants to remember. Not who said what, and never any figures. Over the years these lines become the family's chronicle.

Then finish the meal. The conversation is over when the table is cleared.

## Later conversations

After the first Kitchen Table, a family can return as often as it likes: weekly beside the shopping list, a few times a year, or at turning points such as a first job, a move or a death. Each later conversation can take one theme:

- **The weekly shop and the weekly wage:** looking at what comes in as well as what goes out. See [The Combined Wage](combined-wage.md).
- **Money memories:** the younger generation interviews the older. See [Money Memories](money-memories.md).
- **The ledger:** deciding what the family records and what it leaves unrecorded. See [The Family Ledger](family-ledger.md).
- **What we pass on:** choosing on purpose what the family carries forward. See [The Heirloom](heirloom.md).
- **A decision ahead:** a purchase, a debt, a change of work, talked through before it is made rather than after.

## If it goes badly

Some Kitchen Tables will be awkward. That is normal: many families have never talked about money together. Silence is allowed, and so is laughing.

If the conversation turns to blame or old grievances, pause it and return to the meal. Try again another day with a gentler round, such as memories only. Where money is tied to serious conflict, a mediator or counsellor may be needed before the family can talk it through at home.

## For groups

Schools and community groups can run a Kitchen Table with a shared meal and mixed ages, using the first and third rounds. Skip round two: it is about a particular household and belongs at home. End by giving everyone the three rounds of questions to take back to their own table.
