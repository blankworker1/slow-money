# Slow Work: The Art of Household Management

*A handbook of practice. Third in the series, following "Rebuilding Reciprocity" and "Oikonomos".*

---

## Purpose of this handbook

The first document in this series argued why the household matters. The second described the role of the one who stewards it. This handbook describes the practice: what a well-run household actually does, day by day and year by year.

It is written in the spirit of the old household manuals, of which Isabella Beeton's *Book of Household Management* (1861) is the best known. Unlike them, it does not prescribe recipes or assign tasks by gender or rank. It describes general practices that any household can adapt to its own place, size and circumstances.

Household management is an art rather than a science. Aristotle counted it among the forms of practical wisdom (*phronesis*): judgement built through practice, learned by doing, correction and example. No handbook can replace that. What a handbook can do is name the practices, explain why they matter, and give a household somewhere to start.

## Part I: Foundations

### The household's nomos

The *-nomos* in oikonomos comes from *nemein*, to allot or distribute, which is also the root of *nomos*: custom, rule, law. Every household has its nomos, whether or not it is written down. It is the set of customs that govern how the household lives: who does what, how decisions are made, how things are shared, what is expected of members and guests, and what is never done.

A good nomos is:

- **Known** to every member, including the young.
- **Fitted** to this household, not borrowed wholesale from elsewhere.
- **Revisable**, as members arrive, grow up, age and leave.
- **Consistent**, so that the same situation meets the same response.

Much of the art of household management lies in building good customs, because good customs do the work that would otherwise need constant decisions.

### Ethos: the character of the household

Stewarding a household is not only stewarding things. People need stewarding too.

The Greek *ethos* originally meant an accustomed place, a habit, before it came to mean character. Aristotle held that character is formed by habituation: people become generous by practising generosity, careful by practising care. A household is where most of that habituation happens.

The character of a household is the sum of how its members behave: toward each other, toward guests, toward neighbours, toward the land and the things in their care. It is shaped by the nomos, by example, and above all by what is repeated daily.

### Reputation: the household's good name

Reputation is not strictly part of the nomos. It is the outward reflection of it: how others read the household's customs and character from what they see.

The Greeks had several words for it: *doxa* (how one is regarded), *eukleia* (good repute), *timē* (honour). Xenophon's *Oeconomicus* begins with Socrates seeking out Ischomachus precisely because everyone in Athens calls him a good and honourable man, and Socrates wants to know how such a reputation is earned. The answer the dialogue gives is: through the way he runs his household.

For a household that lives by reciprocity, reputation has a practical weight. Credit between neighbours rests on trust, and trust rests on a record of keeping one's word. A good name is the household's collateral. It is built slowly by reliability, fairness and generosity, and lost quickly by a single broken obligation.

## Part II: The practices

### 1. Order

Xenophon has Ischomachus describe a Phoenician merchant ship on which every item was stowed in its place and the mate could find anything at once, even in a storm. His point was that order is both useful and beautiful.

**In practice:**

- Keep a fixed place for everything the household depends on: tools, stores, documents, spare parts, medicines, keys.
- Label shelves, containers and drawers so that anyone, not only the person who arranged them, can find things.
- Keep an inventory of stores and equipment, and update it as things are used or added.
- Return things after use. Most household disorder begins with one item not put back.
- Review the whole house at least once a year: what is kept, what is no longer needed, what should be passed on.

Order is also a form of redundancy. A household where only one person knows where things are depends on that person.

### 2. The ledger

The household ledger records what the household has and what flows through it. Unlike a bank statement, it counts what money does not.

**A useful ledger has four parts:**

- **Money**: income, obligations and spending, as in any budget.
- **Goods**: what the household produces, stores, uses, gives and receives.
- **Work**: time given to household production, repair and care, including unpaid work.
- **Obligations**: what is owed to others and by others, in favours, loans and help, kept lightly and without insisting on settlement.

**In practice:**

- Keep it simple enough to maintain. A notebook kept weekly is better than a spreadsheet abandoned after a month.
- Review it monthly for patterns, and yearly for the larger picture.
- Use it to learn what enough is for this household. After a few years, the ledger shows what the household actually needs in each season.
- Keep it where every adult member can read it.

### 3. Provisioning and stores

A household's stores are its first protection against bad seasons, lost income and disrupted supply.

**Food stores:**

- Provision staples in season: first from what the household grows or gathers, then through exchange with neighbours, and only then by buying, when they are at their cheapest and best. Store them properly.
- Rotate: first in, first out. Use the oldest stock first and replace from the back.
- Learn several methods of preservation: drying, salting, fermenting, pickling, bottling, curing, cold storage. Do not rely on a single method, especially one that depends on continuous electricity.
- Keep a reserve sized to the household's own judgement of enough, and review it against the ledger.

**Other stores:**

- Water, and the means to collect and treat it.
- Fuel and energy, including stored heat, wood, and battery or solar capacity where available.
- Basic medicines and first-aid supplies.
- Seeds, for next season's growing.
- Spare parts, fastenings, fabric, thread and materials for repair.
- A reserve of money, for obligations that can only be met in money.

Stores should be linked to the network. A household that knows its neighbours' stores, and lets them know its own, turns separate reserves into shared resilience.

### 4. Household production

The household is a producer as well as a consumer. Every return of production to the home reduces dependence on distant markets and strengthens the balance of production and consumption.

**Common forms of household production:**

- **Food**: growing, foraging, keeping animals, preserving, baking, brewing.
- **Energy**: gathering wood, generating and storing power, managing heat.
- **Repair**: mending clothes, tools, furniture, vehicles and buildings.
- **Making**: building, sewing, woodwork, metalwork, whatever the household's skills allow.
- **Care**: raising children, caring for the sick and the old, which is production of the most important kind.

Start with what the place and the household's skills allow, and grow from there. Not every household can grow food, but every household can mend something.

### 5. Maintenance

Good stewardship means handing things on in at least the condition they were received. Maintenance is how that happens.

**In practice:**

- Keep a maintenance calendar for the house, tools, equipment and land: what needs checking, cleaning, servicing or replacing, and when.
- Repair early. A small repair done now prevents a large one later.
- Care for the soil as carefully as for the buildings: feed it, rest it, protect it from erosion.
- Keep records of what was done and when, so that others can continue the work.

### 6. Distribution

*Nemein*, the root of oikonomos, means to allot. Distribution within the household is one of the oldest parts of the art.

**In practice:**

- Distribute according to need, not according to contribution or status. A child, an invalid and an elder all receive what they need.
- Make the principles of distribution known, so that they are seen to be fair.
- Share scarce things openly when they are scarce, rather than letting the strongest take first.
- Give each member some things that are their own to decide on, however small.
- Keep a share for guests and for neighbours in need.

### 7. The people

People need stewarding too: not managing, but care, formation and respect.

**Contribution.** Every member contributes according to ability. The young contribute in small ways from an early age, because contribution is how belonging is learned. The old contribute knowledge, patience and memory, and should never be made to feel like a burden.

**Formation.** Character is formed by habit. The household teaches by what it repeats: how members speak to each other, how they treat guests, whether they keep their word, whether they finish what they start. Example teaches more than instruction.

**Rest.** A household that exhausts its members is not well stewarded. Rest, leisure and celebration belong in the household's rhythm as much as work does.

**Conflict.** Disagreements are normal. A good household settles them early, openly, and according to its customs, before they harden into resentment.

**Guests.** The ancient obligation of hospitality (*xenia*) bound host and guest in mutual duties. The host shelters and feeds; the guest respects the household's customs and does not overstay. Hospitality extends the household's reciprocity beyond its walls.

### 8. The household council

A household governs itself. How it does so is part of its nomos.

**In practice:**

- Hold a regular household meeting, weekly or monthly, where members review the ledger, plan the coming period and raise concerns.
- Include every member old enough to understand, and give the young a real voice in matters that concern them.
- Make large decisions together: major purchases, debts, moves, changes to who lives in the household.
- Record decisions and customs in the household book (see section 12).

### 9. Neighbours and reciprocity

The household does not stand alone. Its security depends on the households around it.

**In practice:**

- Know your neighbours, and let them know you.
- Lend tools, time and help, and accept help in return.
- Share surplus: the glut of courgettes, the extra firewood, the skill someone lacks.
- Keep credit between neighbours open. Not every favour needs to be returned at once, and some never need to be returned at all.
- Match the form of exchange to the relationship: gift and open credit with close neighbours, barter or bounded currencies with more distant ones.
- In a crisis, help first and account later.
- Guard the household's good name by keeping every promise made to others.

### 10. Money

Money remains necessary, because taxes, rent, bills and some goods can only be paid for in money. The art lies in keeping money in its place: a means between uses, not the goal.

**In practice:**

- Earn what the household's money obligations require, and recognise when enough has been earned.
- Budget in the ledger, alongside goods, work and obligations, not separately from them.
- Avoid interest-bearing debt wherever possible, and pay it down where it exists.
- Keep a money reserve for the obligations that nothing else can meet.
- Prefer buying from neighbours and local producers when the choice is available.
- Know the legal forms of non-monetary exchange where you live, and use them properly.

### 11. Rhythms

A household lives in cycles. Many practices belong to a particular cycle, and keeping to the rhythm prevents them from being forgotten.

| Cycle | Typical practices |
| --- | --- |
| **Daily** | Meals, tidying, putting things back, tending animals and plants, checking energy and water |
| **Weekly** | Provisioning, cleaning, ledger entries, household meeting, rest day |
| **Monthly** | Ledger review, stock check of stores, small maintenance, settling obligations |
| **Seasonal** | Sowing, harvesting, preserving, preparing for heat or cold, seasonal maintenance |
| **Annual** | Full ledger review, inventory of house and stores, major maintenance, revision of the household book, celebrations |
| **Generational** | Teaching the young, learning from elders, handing on land, tools, knowledge and customs |

Every household will fill this table differently. The point is to have one.

### 12. The household book

The most useful household manual is not this one. It is the one each household writes for itself.

A household book records:

- The household's customs and principles.
- Where things are kept.
- How essential systems work: water, heat, energy, the garden, the vehicle.
- Recipes, preservation methods and skills particular to this household and place.
- The maintenance calendar and its records.
- Contacts: neighbours, trusted tradespeople, those the household owes and is owed by.
- Decisions made in household council.

The household book serves redundancy above all. If any member were absent tomorrow, another should be able to open the book and carry on. It is also the household's inheritance: the practical knowledge handed from one generation to the next.

## Part III: Learning the art

The art of household management is learned the way crafts have always been learned.

- **By apprenticeship.** The young learn by working beside those who know, not by being told.
- **By repetition.** Skill comes from doing the same things many times, through many seasons.
- **By correction.** Mistakes are part of learning. A spoiled batch of preserves teaches more than a book.
- **From elders.** Much practical knowledge now survives only in the memories of the old. Seek it out while it can still be learned.
- **By sharing.** Every member should learn every essential practice well enough to carry it out. Shared competence is what makes a household resilient.

No household does all of this at once. Begin with what matters most for your place and circumstances: perhaps order, perhaps the ledger, perhaps a store of food. Add practices as they become habits. The art grows with the household.

---

*A household is not run by rules alone, but by customs made good through long practice. The art of household management is the slow work of making them.*
