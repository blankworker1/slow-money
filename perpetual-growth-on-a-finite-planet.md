# Perpetual Growth on a Finite Planet

Sep 30, 2026 · @Carl

## Impossible, and taken for granted

Perpetual economic growth on a finite planet is impossible. It is also the one assumption almost every economic institution treats as beyond question.

Both halves of that sentence can be defended with ordinary evidence. The first rests on physics and arithmetic: you cannot have infinite growth inside a finite container. The second rests on how governments, central banks and markets actually plan, measure and borrow.

The interesting question is not whether the contradiction exists. It is why a belief this easy to disprove is held so firmly, and who pays for holding it.

## The given

No textbook states "the economy must grow forever" as an axiom. It does not need to. The assumption is built into the way the system measures itself and plans ahead.

- **Success is growth.** GDP growth is the headline number of every economy. A good year is one where it rose; a bad year is one where it didn't.
- **Failure is its absence.** A recession is, in practice, two quarters without growth. An economy that holds steady is described as stagnant, never as stable.
- **Debt depends on it.** Government debt is judged sustainable when the economy grows faster than the interest owed. Remove growth and most national debts become unpayable on paper.
- **The future is booked in advance.** Pension funds, budget forecasts and infrastructure plans project growth decades ahead. Their numbers only add up if it arrives.
- **Politics is organised around it.** Parties of left and right disagree about how to share growth, almost never about whether to pursue it.

The economist Kenneth Boulding is widely credited with the line that anyone who believes exponential growth can go on forever in a finite world is either a madman or an economist. The joke lands because the assumption is so rarely examined. It is not argued for. It is simply the water the system swims in.

## The impossible

Every unit of economic activity grounds out in the physical world. Farming needs soil and water. Factories need energy and materials. Even software runs on data centres, cables, chips and electricity. There is no economy outside physics.

Now apply the growth rate the system expects. At 3% a year, an economy doubles roughly every 23 years. After 100 years it is about 19 times larger. After 300 years it is about 7,000 times larger. Nobody seriously proposes a planet extracting, burning and building 7,000 times what it does today. Yet that is where a steady 3% leads, and the doctrine sets no end date.

Imagine a doctor telling an adult patient they must grow 3% larger every year to stay healthy. In a body, growth without limit has another name. In an economy, it is called a sound forecast.

### The escape route, and why it closes

The serious defence is that GDP measures value, not tonnage. Software, services and design can create more value from fewer materials. Some rich countries have cut emissions while their economies grew. This is called decoupling, and it is real.

But it cannot carry the weight placed on it, for three reasons.

1. **Efficiency has a floor.** You can make a process more efficient, but never infinitely so. Physics sets minimum energy costs for heating, moving and computing. Each gain is smaller than the last.
2. **Efficiency invites more use.** When something becomes cheaper to produce, we usually consume more of it. Economists call this the Jevons paradox, after the 19th-century observation that more efficient steam engines increased total coal use.
3. **Much decoupling is exported.** Rich countries often look cleaner because heavy industry moved abroad. Global material extraction has kept rising.

So growth in money value can pull partly away from growth in physical use, for a while. It cannot pull away completely, forever. "Perpetual" is the word that makes the claim impossible.

## Why it is believed anyway

A belief this fragile survives because the system cannot function without it. The reason lies in how money is made.

Most money in circulation is not printed. It is created when a bank makes a loan: the borrower's account is credited with new money, and the bank records an equal debt. When the loan is repaid, that money disappears. The Bank of England described this plainly in its 2014 bulletin on money creation.

Follow the consequence. Every euro exists because someone owes it back, plus interest. If new lending slows, repayments shrink the money supply. A shrinking money supply makes debts harder to pay. Defaults shrink it further. Irving Fisher named this spiral debt deflation after the crash of 1929.

So the system has only two states: expanding or contracting, and contraction threatens to feed on itself. There is no comfortable resting point. Growth is not one policy choice among many. It is the condition the system needs in order to stay upright.

That is why the belief is held so firmly. It is not really a belief about the planet. It is a requirement of the money, projected onto the planet.

## How it is maintained

When real growth gets harder, the requirement does not go away. It is met with credit instead. The sequence runs like this:

1. **Real growth slows**, as physical limits, ageing populations or saturated markets bite.
2. **Credit expands to compensate.** Central banks cut interest rates and buy assets, making borrowing cheaper and more plentiful.
3. **Nominal demand is propped up.** Spending, incomes and tax receipts keep rising in money terms, so existing debts can still be serviced.
4. **Asset prices inflate.** Much of the new credit flows into housing, shares and land, which are not counted in GDP at all.

The result keeps the system upright without resolving the contradiction. Debt grows faster than the real economy beneath it, and the gap is carried forward.

### Fighting technology

There is a second force the system must hold back. Technology naturally makes things cheaper. Computing power per euro has multiplied many times over. Solar panels fell by roughly 90% in price during the 2010s. Artificial intelligence is now doing the same to whole categories of work.

In a system that did not need growth, those gains would reach people as falling prices. Savings would buy more each year. But falling prices are dangerous to a debt-based system, because they make every existing debt heavier in real terms. So central banks target 2% inflation, not zero, and treat price falls as a threat to be prevented.

The effect is a quiet redirection. Technology's gains are not destroyed. They are captured first by whoever is closest to new credit: banks, governments, large borrowers and asset owners. Economists call this the Cantillon effect, after the 18th-century writer who noticed that new money enriches those who spend it first.

## Who pays

**Savers pay first.** Money kept in the bank loses value by design. A 2% target halves purchasing power in about 35 years. Prudence is punished, and every household that wants to protect its labour is pushed into speculation.

**The young pay next.** They arrive after decades of cheap credit have inflated the price of homes and land. They must borrow more, for longer, to own less than their parents did. The debt that keeps the system growing lands on those with the least say in creating it.

**The land pays most.** A system that must grow treats every forest, coastline and hillside as output not yet realised. Extraction is not a side effect of the doctrine; it is how the quota is met. Communities that simply want to stay as they are find themselves called obstacles to development.

The costs are spread thin across people and generations. The benefits are concentrated and immediate. That imbalance is why those who run the system so rarely question it.

## The honest objections

An argument that cannot face its critics is a sermon. Defenders of growth would make four serious points.

**Growth has lifted billions out of poverty.** True. The expansion of the last two centuries fed, housed and educated people on a scale no earlier system managed. But the case here is not that growth was never useful. It is that growth cannot be perpetual, and a system that requires it to be will eventually break against that fact.

**Mild inflation keeps the economy flexible.** Wages rarely fall in money terms, so a little inflation lets real wages adjust without pay cuts. It also gives central banks room to cut rates in a crisis. Both are true, and both are arguments for managing a debt-laden system, not for the idea that it can grow forever.

**Deflation is dangerous.** Japan's long stagnation and the 1930s are the standard warnings. But what those episodes show is the danger of *debt* deflation: the collapse of an over-leveraged system. That is a different thing from the gentle, productivity-driven price falls that technology offers. Treating the two as one is the doctrine's most useful confusion.

**The growth imperative is overstated.** Many economists argue that interest paid to banks circulates back into the economy, so a non-growing money supply is arithmetically possible. That may be so in principle. In practice, every institution described above has been built to assume growth, and none has a plan for its absence.

None of these objections answers the central question: how compound claims can be honoured forever by a finite world.

## Enough

The contradiction will not resolve itself. Either the money changes, or the planet is made to fit the money.

Changing the money does not mean choosing poverty. It means a unit that does not have to grow: fixed in supply, or at least not issued as interest-bearing debt. In such a system, technology's gains reach everyone as lower prices. Saving makes sense again. Growth becomes a choice made when it is useful, not a compulsion to fend off collapse.

Bitcoin is one serious attempt at such money: a fixed issuance schedule and no authority able to expand it to rescue the overextended. Whether it works at scale is still an open question, and it raises hard questions of its own. But it has put the assumption that money must expand to a public test, outside the permission of any central bank.

There are older answers too. Households, cooperatives and communities that trade in kind, share tools and hold land in common have long known how to live well without compound growth. They call it sufficiency. The doctrine calls it stagnation.

A finite planet does not need infinite growth. It needs money honest enough to tell us when we have enough.
