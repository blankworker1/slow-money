# Unenumerability: Becoming Economically Useless

*A manifesto for leaving the measurement game*

A working life is usually told as a story about earning. Forty years, some eighty thousand hours, converted into a salary, then a pension, then whatever is left over. Each stage gets cast as a step toward security. It's a comfortable story because it makes the hours look as though they were exchanged for something solid.

It's also the wrong story, or at least the wrong protagonist. The hours were real. What they were exchanged for was a number in someone else's record, a number that can be created by keystroke, expanded by policy, and thinned out while you sleep.

Nobody built this to hurt you. It is a game. And like every game, it runs only while the players agree that the score is the point.

You can stop agreeing.

## From illegible to unenumerable

A transaction is legible when it can be observed and attributed by someone who was not present at the exchange. That is what a ledger is for. It is also why the ledger, more than the coin, has always been where the contest over value is fought.

Most people who sense this reach first for illegibility. They pay in cash. They swap instead of selling. They keep what they can out of the record. It works, and it is worth doing, but it is a tactic. It is defined by what it avoids, and practised with one eye on whoever holds the pen. You are still in the game, playing defence.

There is another direction. To be unenumerable is to deal in things an absent party could not count even with a clear view of them. Illegibility says: you cannot see me. Unenumerability says: your eyes are not the right instrument.

## What cannot be counted from a distance

**Wealth that does not convert.** A meal grown and eaten at the same table. The land beneath it sits in a registry and gets a tax bill. The supper does not.

**Presence.** An afternoon beside someone who is dying. An hour given whole to a child. It builds no career, it cannot be scaled, and it is the most valuable thing in the room.

**Making without product.** Bread that is eaten. A song that fades into the air. A tool repaired and lent next door. The output is real. The product never existed.

**Need without scarcity.** This place, these people, enough. Minimalism is still a style of shopping. This is a satisfaction that was never indexed to the next purchase.

**Skill without credentials.** The people you have helped know exactly what you can do. An institution has no column for it.

None of this is hidden. All of it happens in plain sight. It simply has no unit.

## But can you eat?

Every version of this argument has to answer that question, and most answer it badly: with a fantasy of total self-sufficiency, or with a shrug.

The honest answer starts with a correction. The economy of gift and mutual aid is not free of accounts. Anyone who has lived in a village knows it keeps them, and forgets nothing. Who brought wood when you were ill. Who turned up for the harvest, and who did not. You will owe and be owed, constantly.

The difference is where the account is kept. It lives in the memory of the people who were there. It cannot be copied, sold, aggregated, taxed or foreclosed on, because it does not exist outside the relationship that holds it. Nobody absent can read it. **Unenumerable does not mean unaccounted for. It means named, and never numbered.**

So yes, you can eat. You eat the way people have eaten for most of history: because you are woven into other people's survival, and they into yours. That is work. In some ways it is more work than a salary, since turning up cannot be automated or deferred.

The second correction is about purity. Nobody exits whole. The land has its tax bill. The dentist wants paying. Some of what feeds any informal economy is surplus from the formal one. To pretend otherwise turns a good idea into a pose.

The practical version is a matter of proportion. Each year, a little more of what you need comes from people who know you, and a little less from a number an institution holds on your behalf. One meal, one skill, one favour at a time.

You have already started. Every lift given, every child minded, every dinner cooked for friends was unenumerable. The task is to let that part of your life grow until the counted part is the smaller one.

And as it grows, the levers lose their reach. Inflation takes less from someone who holds less of what is being inflated. Losing a job is no verdict on someone whose worth was never the wage. You are not immune. You are less exposed. A bank failure starts to look like weather.

## What it costs

You give up some of the comforts of the counted: easy credit, the credential that opens the door, the quick answer to "and what do you do?". People who can only see the scoreboard will call you lazy, or a burden. They are describing their instrument.

The harder costs are material. Illness and old age get carried by people instead of by policy numbers, and people carry you only as far as the bonds you have built. Those bonds cannot be bought late. They are made by years of giving before you ever need to receive.

That is why this is best begun early, slowly, and in company. It is also why it is worth it. What you build this way cannot be debased from a distance. No keystroke reaches it.

## Useless to whom?

"Economically useless" is the measurement's word for whatever it cannot use. Take it as a compliment.

You do not have to burn the numbers. You will need some of them for a while yet. Just stop mistaking them for the thing they count.

Be indispensable to a handful of people and invisible to the tally. Feed and be fed. Owe and be owed, by name, face to face. Let the score go on being kept by those who still think it is the point.

That is all it takes to become economically useless.

*Enough is not a number.*
