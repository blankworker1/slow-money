# No Names, No Numbers #1

## I. The Pause

At 07:42 the persona woke against her face and showed her the day.

It was a thin film, warm as skin, that every citizen wore in public from the age of six. To the cameras it displayed her code, RSSLNE-0412-K, and a narrow green line that meant compliant. To her it displayed everything else. Temperature 14 degrees. Balance 1,206.40. Credit standing 742, up 3. Steps yesterday 6,118. Shift begins 08:30. Bakery queue: 4 persons, estimated wait 2 minutes 10 seconds.

She had bought bread from the same bakery for 11 years. The baker was a broad woman whose persona read GRBMRA-1957-T, and who said, every morning, "Good morning, citizen," because that was the greeting the persona logged as courteous.

That morning something was different. A man behind her in the queue said the baker's code aloud, the way people did when they wanted to be served next, and the baker did not turn. Not at once. There was a small space, the length of a breath, and then she looked up and smiled and said, "One moment, citizen."

Nobody else noticed. Everyone else answered their code at once. It was not a rule. It was simply what a head did when it heard its code: it turned. The persona measured it. Response latency 0.21 seconds, well within the normal band.

She found herself watching for it after that. Over the following 9 days she saw it 4 times: in the baker, in an old man at the tram stop, in a girl stacking crates outside the market. A breath of nothing before the answer. As if the code had to travel a long way to reach them.

On the 10th day, as the baker handed her the loaf, she pressed a second, smaller loaf into her hands, wrapped in paper. Not scanned. No price on the persona.

"You've been watching," the baker said quietly. "That's good. Most people never see it."

"See what?"

The baker only smiled. "Bring that back to me unopened, and I'll take you somewhere."

She walked to work with the small loaf against her chest. Her persona showed nothing about it at all. It was the first thing she had held in years that the persona could not see.

At her desk, at 08:29, a colleague called her code across the room. She heard herself wait, the length of a breath, before she turned.

## II. The Basket

She brought the loaf back unopened. The baker weighed it in her hand, as if she could tell from the weight alone whether it had been opened, and nodded.

"Tomorrow," she said. "When the street lights come on."

"What time is that?"

The baker looked at her with something like pity. "When the lights come on."

There was no address. The baker walked her there the first time, through streets she had crossed a thousand times without seeing. Past the fountain that had no water in it. Left at the wall where someone had once painted a horse, now only a pale shape of a horse. Down the lane that smelled of the soap works, to the house where a fig tree had grown up through the yard and split the wall in two.

"Next time you'll find it yourself," the baker said. "Remember the horse, not the street."

The door opened onto a small hallway and a deep wicker basket on the floor. Inside it lay what everyone carries: a handset, a wristband, a payment card, the grey identity sleeve. The baker took off her wristband and laid it in. Then she reached up to her face, found the edge of the persona with her fingernail, and peeled it away.

She had not seen an unmasked face in public since she was a child. It was against the law, and worse, it was indecent: like undressing in the street. The baker's face underneath was older than her persona had shown, lined at the eyes, a small scar through one eyebrow. It looked at her as nothing had looked at her for years.

"Everything in the basket," the baker said gently. "It stays at the door. It isn't welcome further in."

Her hands shook. She put in the handset, the card, the sleeve. Then she reached for her own persona. It came away with a small, soft sound, like a plaster lifted from a healed cut, and the cold air of the hallway touched her cheeks.

No display. No code, no balance, no time. For a moment she felt she had gone blind. Then she understood that she was simply seeing, and that it had been a very long time.

Beyond the hallway was a kitchen, warm and loud, and full of faces. They turned to look at her, but slowly, and they did not look at her the way the cameras did, for something to read. They looked at her the way you look at someone who has come in out of the cold.

"This is the one who came with the baker," someone said. And that, for now, was who she was.

## III. The Bread

The table was laid a little wider than the company, so there was a place for her without anyone having to make one. At its head sat an old man with hands like roots, and when everyone had settled he lifted the loaf she had carried, the one she had brought back unopened, and broke it.

The kitchen went quiet.

"We keep no names here," the old man said, looking at her. "And no numbers. That is all you need to learn tonight. The rest you'll learn by sitting here."

The bread went round. Hands took what they wanted. There was soup, and a dish of beans with oil and garlic, and a hard cheese someone had carried in their coat. Nobody served portions. Nobody asked whether there was enough. There was.

She wanted to understand, and so she asked the question she would have asked anywhere. "Are those the rules? How many are there?"

The old man smiled at her with great kindness. "No, thank you," he said.

That was all. Nobody laughed. The talk at the table carried on around her: about the rain, about a roof that leaked, about someone's daughter who had started to walk. She sat with her face burning and her question still in her mouth, and slowly she began to listen to how they spoke.

They said: *after the swallows came back*. They said: *before your tea goes cold*. They said: *a good while*, *not long*, *as much again*. A woman explained how she made the beans: a handful, then oil until it looked right, then garlic until you were sure, and then a little more. A man described a walk he had taken: past the second bridge, no, past the bridge with the broken rail, until the path turned to sand.

It should have been confusing. It was not. Everyone understood well enough. When the roof was mentioned, someone said they had slates to spare, and nobody asked how many. When the daughter was mentioned, no one asked her age; they asked whether she laughed when she fell.

She realised she had not once wondered what time it was.

The meeting did not end. It simply came to its own end, the way a fire does. When the bread was gone, people began to stand, and kiss cheeks, and take their coats, and drift towards the hallway and the basket.

The next time she came she brought apricots, and she was proud of them. "There are twenty-four," she said, setting the bowl down. "I counted, so there's enough for everyone."

The old man at the head of the table smiled at her. "No, thank you," he said, kindly, and took one.

Nobody looked at her. The bowl went round, and every hand took what it wanted, and when it came back to her there was one left, as if it had been waiting. Walking home, she realised no one had needed to know how many there were. There had been enough. That was all anyone had ever needed to know.

The time after that, they spoke of her as the one who brought the apricots.

## IV. The Account

That winter the heating in her block was cut back to the permitted minimum, and her persona began to show her credit standing in amber.

She said nothing about it at the table. It seemed like exactly the kind of thing that would earn her a "No, thank you." But the one who mended the chair noticed her hands, and how she held them round her cup, and asked her plainly: "Are you cold at home?"

She hesitated. Then she said it the way she had heard others say things. "I'm short of warmth."

Nobody said anything more about it. But the next time she came, there was a bundle of split wood in the hallway beside the basket, tied with string, and a blanket folded on top of it. Nobody claimed it. When she asked who had brought it, people looked at her pleasantly and talked about something else.

She carried it home and burned the wood in the old stove nobody used, and slept warm for the first time in a long while. And all the next week the old reflex worked at her. She had been given something. She must owe something. How much? To whom? By when? She lay awake composing the sentence she would say.

At the table she said it. "I want to repay whoever brought the wood. What do I owe?"

"No, thank you," said the old man, and passed her the salt.

It was the woman who made the beans who took pity on her afterwards, in the hallway, while they were putting their personas back on.

"You're trying to keep a book," she said. "We don't keep one. That doesn't mean nobody knows anything." She smiled. "Everyone knows who brought slates when the roof leaked. Everyone knows who sat up with the old man when he was ill, and who was too busy. That's remembered. It's just never added up. Nobody could tell you what anyone owes. It's not that sort of knowing."

"So I don't owe anything?"

"You'll know when it's your turn. You'll see someone who's short of something you have. You won't need to be asked."

It happened before the summer. A young man who had come with the one who mended the chair said, quietly, that he was short of shoes. She had a pair of her brother's in a cupboard, good ones, unworn since he went away. She brought them the next time and left them in the hallway, and said nothing.

Later, much later, she tried to thank the one who had brought the wood. She had worked out at last who it was: a quiet man who always sat near the stove. She found him in the hallway and began to say it.

He looked at her with real puzzlement. "Wood?" he said. Then, kindly: "Was it cold that winter? I don't remember."

She understood then that he was not pretending. He had given it in the way you would put out a hand to someone stumbling, and then forgotten it, because there was nothing to remember. That was the gift. Not the wood. The forgetting.

## V. The Rememberer

She recognised the girl at once, though she had only seen her through a persona: the girl from the market, stacking crates, who had waited a breath before answering her code. Unmasked she was younger than she had looked, with a wide mouth and ink on her fingers that would not wash off.

She was the Rememberer.

When the bread had gone round and the soup was poured, the table went quiet, and the girl began to speak. Not to read, there was nothing to read from, but to say, in a level voice, as if she were telling a story everyone already knew.

*The basket stays at the door, and what is in it is not spoken of at the table.*

*What is said at the table is not carried outside.*

*Whoever brings a guest answers for them, until the guest can answer for themselves.*

*Anyone may leave, and take nothing, because there is nothing to take.*

*A wrong done here is answered here, at the table, and nowhere else.*

She went on, agreement after agreement, some old and plain, some strange: something about the well at the edge of the town and who might draw from it in a dry summer; something about a family who had moved away and whose dog was to be fed by whoever passed. When the last of the bread was eaten, she stopped in the middle of a sentence, and smiled, and reached for the cheese.

"She'll pick it up there next time," the baker murmured. "From the same word."

"Isn't it ever written down?"

The baker gave her a look that was very nearly a "No, thank you." Then she relented. "If it were written, it would be a book. Books get read by people who weren't here. This is only for people who were."

Over the seasons she heard the whole of it, and then heard it again, and she began to find that she knew pieces by heart without trying, the way you know a song you have never set out to learn.

Once, the girl came to an agreement and stopped, frowning. Something about a boundary stone and a field. She began it, and lost it, and began it again. She looked round the table. Nobody could finish it. The old man thought about it for a long moment, and shook his head.

"Then it's done with," he said. "If none of us can carry it, it was too heavy." And the girl went on to the next.

In the spring the girl fell ill, and did not come. The table was quiet when the bread had gone round, and the one who brought the apricots felt a kind of fear: that without the girl the law had gone too.

Then the one who mended the chair said, a little awkwardly, *The basket stays at the door.* And the woman who made the beans said, *And what is in it is not spoken of at the table.* And someone else took up the next, and someone the next, and when they faltered, someone else supplied the missing words, until she heard her own voice among them.

The law had not been kept by the girl. It had been kept by everyone who had listened. The girl had only been its voice.

## VI. The Face

It was the young man with her brother's shoes.

She was waiting for a tram when he passed her in the street, and he must have known her by her walk, or her coat. He was pleased to see her. He called out her code, cheerfully, the way you would call a friend's name, and raised a hand.

She did not pause. She turned at once, as if he were a stranger who had read her code off the display, and looked straight through him, and he faltered and walked on. But the cameras on the tram stop had heard it. Two personas, greeting one another, outside any logged reason to know each other. It was a small thing. Small things were what the system was built to notice.

She did not sleep well that night, or for many after.

At the table she said nothing for a long time. Then, when the bread had gone round, she said, "Something was done that I need answered."

The kitchen went quiet in a way she had not heard before. The old man nodded to her to go on. She told it plainly: the street, the hand, the code called out loud. The young man went red, and then white, and did not try to deny it.

"I wasn't thinking," he said. "I was glad to see you."

"I know," she said. And she found that she did know it, and that it did not make it better.

The old man turned to her. "What would restore your face?"

She had expected to be asked what punishment she wanted, and she had an answer ready: that he be sent away, that he not come again. She opened her mouth to say it and found it was not true. It would not give her anything back. It would only take something from him.

"Not what would hurt him," the old man said, as if he had watched the thought cross her face. "What would make you whole."

She thought for a long while. The table waited. Nobody hurried her.

"He called me by my code," she said at last. "As if that was me. I want him to know my face instead. Properly. So well that he'd never need the code to find me. And then I want him never to look at me outside again. Never. Not even to be kind."

The old man looked round the table. Heads nodded, slowly, one after another. The woman who made the beans said, "That's just." The baker said, "That's just."

So the young man came and sat opposite her, and looked at her face. It was very hard for both of them. He looked until he stopped seeing a stranger and began to see her: the line between her brows when she was tired, the small chip in her front tooth, the way one eye was a little lighter than the other. She looked back at him, because she found she could not not. And somewhere in it he began to cry, quietly, and she did not look away.

When it was done, she felt that it was done. Not forgiven, exactly, though that came later. Settled. Her face had been given back to her, by the very person who had taken it.

Afterwards they spoke of him as the one who looked properly. Outside, in the street, he never turned his head towards her again, and she never needed him to.

## VII. The Delay

The notice arrived at 06:15 on a Tuesday, in amber.

*Citizen RSSLNE-0412-K. Response latency anomaly detected. 30-day average 1.38 seconds (band: 0.15–0.45). Please attend a wellness calibration at Civic Health Unit 7, Thursday, 10:00. Attendance is voluntary. Non-attendance will be noted.*

She read it 3 times. Then she made coffee, and drank it, and went to work, and answered her code at her desk at 08:31 in 0.24 seconds.

The Civic Health Unit was clean and pale and pleasant. A woman in a white persona, whose code was green and whose voice had been tuned to be soothing, sat her in a chair facing a screen. "Nothing to worry about," the woman said. "Many citizens drift a little. Stress. Sleep. We'll just recalibrate. I'm going to call your code at random intervals. Respond as you naturally would."

The first call came. She heard it, and in the space before she turned she saw the whole of it: the kitchen, the basket, the bread, the girl reciting, the young man's wet face, the wood by the door. Everything the system could not see, and everything it was now, very gently, trying to find.

She turned. 0.19 seconds.

The second call. 0.22. The third. 0.20. She answered every one of them at once, cleanly, perfectly, like a head that had never known it could do anything else. The woman in the white persona smiled at the screen. "Well within band. You see? Just stress."

Walking out into the street, she understood what the baker had never needed to explain. The pause had never been in the turning of the head. A head can be trained to turn. The pause was in her, and it was hers, and it did not need to show. She could wear the mask perfectly now, because she knew it was a mask. Nothing the persona measured would ever tell it that.

Somewhere above the city, in a room she would never see, an analyst closed a file. It contained 4,000 citizens whose latency had drifted and then, after calibration, returned to normal. The analyst had once suspected a pattern: shared locations, shared hours, some kind of network. But there were no names to link, no transactions, no messages, nothing written anywhere. Only a statistical shadow, a delay with nothing behind it. In the end the file was given a placeholder name and archived.

They called it *Delayed*.

That evening, when the lights came on, she walked past the fountain with no water in it, left at the wall with the pale shape of a horse, down the lane that smelled of soap, to the house where the fig tree had split the wall.

In the hallway she knelt by the basket. She put in the handset, the card, the sleeve, the wristband with its amber line. Then she found the edge of the persona with her fingernail, and lifted it away, and let the cold air touch her face.

In the kitchen they turned to look at her, slowly. The bread was waiting.

"Here's the one who came back," someone said.

And she was.
