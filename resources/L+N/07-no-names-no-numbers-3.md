# No Names, No Numbers #3: Attention

## I. Too Much

In summer the batteries were full before the bread was cold.

The one who kept the wall would walk down to the shed after breakfast and lay her hand on the casing, the way you test a loaf. Warm. Full. Nothing more would go in. On the roof the panels went on catching the light, and the light went on arriving, and there was nowhere left to put it.

For a long time nobody minded. The fans turned, the fridge hummed, the lamps came on at night. What the panels caught and could not store simply went back into the air, like rain on a field nobody had sown.

It was the one who mended the pump who said it first, one evening, with his feet in the river. "We throw away more than we use," he said. "Every day. Right up until the swallows leave."

Nobody answered. Then the one who kept the wall said, "And in winter we sit in the dark."

That was the beginning. Nobody decided anything. It was only that, once it had been said, it could not be unseen: the surplus in the morning, the shortage in the evening, the long summer of waste and the long winter of want, and between them a gap that was not a lack of sun at all. Only a lack of keeping.

## II. The Gathering

You cannot put light in a jar. That was the first thing they learned, and it took the rest of the summer to learn what to do instead.

The tomatoes went up on the roof. Split, salted, laid on racks between the panels, they shrank and darkened in the heat until they were leather, and then they went into oil. The one who had always grown too many was suddenly the one everyone came to see.

The pump learned to work at noon. When the shed was full, a switch closed, and the river came up the hill into the old tank above the houses. Water that had been low was now high. Nobody had to explain that this was the sun, stored. You could put your hand in the tank in the evening and feel it.

And in the corner of the shed, under the bench, a machine began to hum whenever there was nothing else for the light to do. It was the least beautiful thing they owned. It did nothing anyone could see. It sat in its own heat and turned the wasted hours into something small that kept, and that could be shared, and that would still be there in the dark.

The children called it the cricket, because it only sang when the sun was high.

By the time the figs came, the rhythm had settled into the village without anyone writing it down. When the shed was full, a white cloth went up on the pole by the bakery. That meant: now. Wash now, cook now, run the saw now. The light is free. When the cloth came down, people waited.

On grey days the pole stood bare, and it was strange how much that bare pole said.

## III. The Reading

When the day and the night stood level, the one who kept the wall called everyone to the square.

The wall was the end of the old tannery, where the stone faced south. Years before, someone had cut a line into it at the height a shadow reached at noon on the longest day, and another where it reached on the shortest. Between them the stone was bare. That autumn, for the first time, she cut a third mark, and it was not about shadows.

She had the jars brought out, and the tank was opened so everyone could see how high the water stood, and the one who mended the pump read from the cricket what it had kept. She did not say how much. Nobody would have known what to do with a number. She held a stick against the wall and asked, "Is it this? Or is it this?" and the crowd argued, and pointed, and laughed, until they agreed. Then she cut the line where they had agreed.

It was not exact. It did not need to be. Next year there would be another line, above it or below, and anyone walking past would see which.

"That," she said, tapping the stone, "is what the summer gave us that we would have thrown away."

An old man at the back said it was more than his father's field had ever given him. Nobody could check. Nobody tried.

## IV. The Shortest Day

The one who arrived with the rain had been in the village since the grapes came in. She had a room above the bakery and a job she did on a screen, and nobody had asked her what it was. In the evenings she watched the white cloth on the pole and did not understand why people looked at it the way they did.

On the shortest day she was told to come to the square before noon, and to bring nothing.

The tables were already out along the tannery wall. They were laid wider than the crowd, the way things were laid there, so that whoever came found a place. The jars stood in a row down the middle, oil gleaming. There were loaves, and the last of the figs, and wine from the hill. Nobody was eating.

She sat where a child pointed and waited, like everyone else, with her hands in her lap. The sun was low and pale over the river. The square was half in shadow. It was cold enough to see your breath.

"What are we waiting for?" she asked the woman beside her, quietly.

The woman nodded at the wall.

The shadow of the old iron bracket, the one that had held a tannery sign once, lay on the stone high up, near the top mark, a little to one side of it. It was moving. You could not see it move. But if you looked away and looked back, it was closer.

Then a girl stood on a bench, the one who had named the cricket, and said, in the loud flat voice children use for things they have learned by heart:

> *I am a payment no one collects.* *The more of me you give, the richer you become.* *The giver of all things has never asked for me,* *yet once a year, at its weakest, I am owed.* *What am I?*

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Every face along the table had already turned to the wall.

## V. Paid

She looked because they were looking.

At first there was only the wall, and the shadow, and the cold. Then she lifted her eyes, the way the others had, over the roofs and the river to where the sun sat low above the hill. It was not bright. It was the colour of the oil in the jars. She could look straight at it, almost.

She had not done that since she was a child. She tried to remember the last time and could not. The sun had become the thing behind the weather, behind the windows, behind the screen. Something you were in, never something you looked at.

The shadow touched the mark.

Nobody cheered. Nobody spoke. For a while, long enough to feel long, the whole square sat facing south with nothing in its hands, and the only sound was the river. She felt foolish, and then she did not. She felt the light on her face, thin as it was, and thought about the tomatoes on the roof and the water climbing the hill and the cricket singing in the shed through all those white-cloth days, and understood at last what the cloth meant.

Then the one who kept the wall broke the first loaf, and it was over, and it had begun.

They ate. The jars went round, and every hand took what it wanted. Before the light went, the one who mended the pump walked the length of the tables and put something small beside every plate, hers too, the cricket's share of the summer. She tried to say it was too much, that she had not been there to gather it. He smiled. "No, thank you," he said, and moved on.

The sun went down early, as it does on that day. Nobody went home. The lamps along the wall came on, one after another, lit by a summer none of them could see, and the feast went on in the warm yellow circle they made in the dark.

Later, walking back to her room above the bakery, she passed the tannery wall and stopped. The marks were invisible now. But she knew where they were. And she knew that tomorrow the shadow would pass a little below the highest one, and the day after a little lower, and that the light, which had never been scarce, only unkept, was already on its way back.

She had paid. It had cost her nothing but a minute of looking. And she had never felt so rich.
