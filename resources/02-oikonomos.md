# Oikonomos

*A role description, historical and modern. Second in a series on household economics.*

---

## Purpose of this document

This document describes the role of the *oikonomos*, the steward of the household, as it was understood in antiquity and as it can be understood today. It is written in the form of a role description: what the role is for, whom it answers to, what it requires, and how it is judged.

The oikonomos is not a job title that one person holds. It is a role that a household fills, ideally through several of its members at once.

---

## Part I: The historical role

### The word

*Oikonomos* combines *oikos* (household) and *nemein* (to manage, to distribute, to allot). The oikonomos is the one who manages and apportions the household's resources. From the same root came *oikonomia*, the art of household management, and eventually the modern word "economics".

### In the Greek household

The fullest ancient portrait is Xenophon's *Oeconomicus* (4th century BC). In it, the landowner Ischomachus explains how he trained his young wife to run the household: keeping stores in order, knowing what the house held, supervising work, caring for the sick, and teaching servants. He also describes training an *epitropos*, a bailiff, to manage the estate's work in his absence.

Aristotle, in the first book of the *Politics*, set oikonomia against *chrematistike*, the art of acquiring wealth. Oikonomia aims at use and has a natural limit. Chrematistics, when it becomes its own end, has none. The oikonomos was the practitioner of the first and the guard against the second.

### In the Roman household

The Roman equivalent on the farm was the *vilicus*, the estate manager, usually a slave or freedman. Cato's *De Agri Cultura* lists his duties: keeping order, maintaining tools and buildings, knowing the stores, not lending or borrowing without authority, and rendering account to the owner.

Roman law also gave a precise form to the idea behind stewardship. *Usufruct* is the right to use something and enjoy its fruits *salva rerum substantia*: without impairing its substance. The one who holds in usufruct may live from the yield but must hand the source on intact.

### In later usage

By the time of the New Testament, *oikonomos* simply means "steward": one who manages what is not wholly theirs and must give an account of it. The parable of the unjust steward in Luke turns on exactly this accountability.

### Two lessons from history

**Fixed roles created fragility as well as inequality.** The ancient household assigned stewardship by gender and status, and relied on slave and servant labour. This was unjust, and it was also brittle. Knowledge concentrated in one person left the household exposed when that person died, fell ill or left.

**The extension to larger scales was a disguise.** From the early modern period, the household image was applied to kingdoms and states: *économie politique*, mercantilism, cameralism. In practice these aimed at accumulating treasure and revenue. This was chrematistics under the household's name. A city is not a household, and its administrator is not an oikonomos.

## Part II: The modern role

### Role summary

The oikonomos holds the household in trust. The role ensures that the household has what it needs, that its sources of life are kept intact, and that it remains connected to the households around it through reciprocity. It is measured by the condition of what it hands on, not by the size of what it takes.

### Accountable to

1. **The household first**: its present members, who share its table and its fate.
2. **Its past**: those who built and handed on the land, the skills and the obligations it holds.
3. **Its future**: those who will inherit it.
4. **Its neighbours**: the households whose reciprocity it depends on and contributes to.

The oikonomos is not accountable to a market, an investor or an administrator.

### Foundational understandings

The role rests on three understandings. Everything else follows from them.

**1. Stewardship.** Stewardship is not maximisation. Stewarding land is not the same as maximising the harvest. The steward lives from the yield and leaves the source intact: soil as fertile, tools maintained, skills passed on, relationships unbroken. Yield is a result of good stewardship, not its goal.

**2. The unit.** The oikonomos must understand who and what the household is, and where its boundaries lie. The unit is whoever shares the table and the fate, and may include elders, dependents, long-term guests, and the land, animals and tools the household depends on. The boundary decides where generalised reciprocity applies. It changes over time and must be understood continuously, not defined once.

**3. Enough.** The hardest understanding, and one that cannot be calculated in advance. It is learned and strengthened by:

- **Time**, which reveals the cycles, the lean years and the variance that enough must allow for.
- **The ledger**, which turns a feeling into knowledge of what the household actually uses.
- **Stores**, which make restraint possible, because a secure household can stop without fear.
- **Stewardship across past, present and future**: enough is what the present can take without drawing on what the past handed on or the future will need.

### Key responsibilities

**Keeping enough.** Hold production and consumption in balance at household scale. Recognise when needs are met, and decline the extra effort, debt or acquisition the household does not need.

**Keeping the ledger.** Know what the household has and what flows through it, including what money does not record: food grown, repairs done, care given, tools lent, favours owed and owing.

**Stewarding stores.** Decide what the household holds in reserve (food, fuel, energy, savings, materials, skills), maintain it, and keep it linked to the network, so that in a crisis one household's reserve becomes another's support.

**Managing the boundary.** Choose the right mode of exchange for each relationship: gift within the household, open credit among neighbours, barter and bounded currencies between communities, and the global market only for what nothing closer can supply.

**Guarding against chrematistics.** Earn what obligations in state money require, and treat money as a means between uses rather than an end. Avoid and reduce interest-bearing debt, which turns part of the household's labour into money that breeds for others.

**Allocating time and energy.** Plan work around what is actually available: daylight, seasons, energy flows, and the capacity of the household's members. Work within bounded abundance, not against it.

**Keeping skills and continuity.** Maintain practical knowledge, pass it on to the young, and relearn it from the old.

**Maintaining redundancy.** Ensure that no essential knowledge or task depends on a single person. Share understanding of the stores, the ledger, the obligations and the reasoning behind decisions.

**Rendering account.** Make the state of the household visible to its members, and answer to them for how it is being stewarded.

### Required competencies

- Practical skill in the household's main forms of production: food, repair, energy, care, making.
- Numeracy sufficient to keep a ledger, including non-monetary flows.
- Knowledge of storage and preservation suited to the household's place and climate.
- Understanding of the household's legal and monetary obligations, and of the legal forms of non-monetary exchange where it lives.
- Judgement in reciprocity: knowing what is owed, what is given, and what should be left open.
- Patience with cycles longer than a year.
- The ability to teach.

### How the role is held

**Shared, not assigned.** The role is held by the household, not by a single post-holder. It may be divided by domain, rotated, or held jointly, but every adult member should be able to step into it.

**Redundant by design.** At least two people should be able to carry out each essential responsibility. A household that depends on one steward has a single point of failure.

**Bounded in scale.** The role exists only where its conditions hold: a steward who shares the household's fate, needs that are directly visible, accountability that is face to face, and members who know each other. Beyond the household, households federate and coordinate through reciprocity. Those who coordinate them are not oikonomoi.

### What the oikonomos is not

| Figure | Relationship to resources | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Owner** | Holds by right, may use up | None required |
| **Consumer** | Spends to meet wants, produces nothing to balance | None |
| **Investor** | Stewards assets toward growth | None; chrematistics in its purest form |
| **Economist** (modern sense) | Allocates scarce means among given, unlimited ends | Excluded by definition |
| **Administrator** | Manages revenue for an entity whose fate they do not share | Set by budget, not by need |
| **Oikonomos** | Holds in trust, lives from the yield, hands on intact | Enough |

### Measures of success

The oikonomos is not measured by growth. The questions are:

- Does the household have what it needs?
- Are its sources of life (land, tools, skills, relationships) in at least as good a condition as when they were received?
- Could the household continue if any one member were absent?
- When a neighbouring household falls short, is this household close enough and connected enough to help?
- Will those who come after be ready to take up the role themselves?

---

*The oikonomos does not own the household. They hold it in trust, live from what it yields, and hand it on intact.*
