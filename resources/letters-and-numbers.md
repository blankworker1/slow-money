# Letters and Numbers

*What a ledger needs, and what a life does not*

Italian recipes use a notation that no accountant would accept. Where the amount should be, beside the salt or the oil, they print two letters: q.b. *Quanto basta.* As much as is enough.

The recipe does not say how much that is. It trusts that whoever is cooking will know. In the one place a number was expected there are letters, and the dish comes out fine.

That small abbreviation holds an argument about money, about records, and about how much of a life needs to be written down at all.

## Every entry has two columns

A ledger entry has two parts. There is a name, and there is an amount. Who, and how much. Take either away and it stops working as an entry.

The two parts do different jobs. The name makes a transaction legible: readable, and attributable to someone, by a party who was not present. The word comes from the Latin for reading. Legibility is a matter of letters.

The amount makes it enumerable: expressed in a unit that can be added to every other amount in the book. Enumeration is a matter of numbers.

Most talk of escaping the ledger runs these together. They are separate, and you can strike out one without touching the other.

## Four ways to keep an account

|  | With a number | Without a number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **With a name** | The ledger | The village account |
| **Without a name** | Cash | No account at all |

**Name and number** is the ledger proper: the bank statement, the tax record, the payslip. Everything in it can be read from a distance and added up.

**Number without a name** is cash. A banknote says exactly how much and nothing about who. A bearer coin does the same, whatever it is made of. This is the illegible economy. It has struck out the letters and kept the numbers.

**Name without a number** is the village account. Everyone knows who brought wood when you were ill and who stayed away at harvest. Nobody could tell you the balance, because it was never in a unit. It is held in memory and has never been added up.

**Neither** is what leaves no account behind. The song that fades. The hour of full attention. The gift both sides forget.

Moving down the table is illegibility. Moving across it is unenumerability. It is tempting to see the second as a step beyond the first. It is better seen as a turn in a different direction, and a life can make both.

## The nonna has no scales

Watch someone who has made the same cake more times than she could tell you. No scales come out. The flour goes in by the handful, the oil by the look of it. The amounts never become quantities at all. The cake feels right, and it tastes delicious.

This is unenumerability in its embodied form. The knowledge is real and exact, and it lives in the hand. Ask the nonna for her recipe and she will say "until it feels right". That is a complete answer to anyone standing beside her, and no answer to anyone else.

Which tells you what the scales are for. They serve the person who was not there: the stranger with the cookbook, the novice with no grandmother. A written recipe is cooking's ledger. Grams do for the absent cook what the ledger's figures do for the absent party. Measurement is what stands in for presence.

Many cooks begin with the scales and put them away once the hand has learned. For them the number was scaffolding. Unenumerability, seen this way, is a kind of fluency. It is what comes after counting, when the same thing has been done many times for the same people.

## Fight the scales

The word has two meanings, and they are one problem. Only what can be weighed can be scaled. A recipe needs grams before a factory can run it, and the factory is why the grams were written down.

Follow that outward. School teaches grams because it cannot supply a grandmother. A credential stands in for the master who would have vouched for you. A credit score stands in for the village that knew your family. Each time, a number arrives where a line of transmission has been cut.

So enumeration is more than an instrument of the powerful. It is what remains when people are separated from those who knew them. Uproot a community and it has to be numbered, because no one is left who can name it.

The scales are a fair crutch for the novice with no nonna. The fight is against needing them for ever, and against any arrangement that removes the nonni and then sells the scales as a substitute.

## Where the number belongs

None of this is an argument against numbers. Bake for two hundred strangers and the scales come back. Trade across distance and time, with people you will never meet, needs an amount both sides can check.

For that remainder the questions are the old ones. Who keeps the count, and who gets to read it? A count that no authority can inflate answers the first. An object that passes from hand to hand without a record answers the second. Both are worth building, and both belong in the left-hand column.

The mistake is to let that column grow until it holds everything. Keep an honest number for strangers, and none for the people at your table.

## Enough is not a number

The number line has no place on it marked enough. Whatever figure you reach, there is another just above it. A life measured in a unit inherits that property. So does an economy, which is how growth without limit came to sound like common sense.

Enough is a judgment, made by someone present, about something particular: this bread, this table, these people. It has a size, and the cook knows it exactly. It simply never becomes a figure.

The recipe had it right all along. Where the amount should be, write q.b., and trust whoever is in the kitchen.

Keep letters for the people who know you and numbers for the people who do not, and let the first list be the longer one. That is the whole of it: letters and numbers.

*Quanto basta.*
