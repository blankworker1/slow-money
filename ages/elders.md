# Slow Money for Elders

*For grandparents, great-aunts and uncles, and the older generation of any family.*

You hold the family's longest view of money. You have seen prices rise and currencies change, lived through good years and hard ones, and learned what could be trusted and what could not. Much of this has never been written down. In Slow Money, your experience is not a thing of the past. It is the family's most valuable knowledge.

## Your memories are knowledge

What you remember about money is local history, family history and practical wisdom all at once: what a week's wages bought, how the family managed when work dried up, where savings were kept and why.

Your grandchildren cannot learn this from anyone else. When you are gone, it goes with you, unless it is told.

**Ask yourself:** What do you know about money that nobody younger in your family knows?

## Being asked

In [Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md), a younger family member will interview you. You decide what to share. You can skip any question and stop at any time, and nothing you say will go beyond the family without your permission.

Some of your memories may be painful: hardship, loss, war, a business that failed. You don't have to tell those stories. But families often learn the most from the hard times, and a story told calmly can teach a grandchild more than any lesson.

## Talking about inheritance

Inheritance is where families most often fall out, and almost always because things were left unsaid. The kindest thing you can do for your family may be to talk about your wishes while you are here to explain them.

This does not have to be about money alone. What should happen to the house, the land, the tools, the family photographs? Who should keep the chronicle? What do you hope your family will never forget?

The family chronicle can record your wishes and your reasons (see [The Family Ledger](../practices/family-ledger.md)). But it has no legal force. Your wishes about property and money should also be set out in a proper will, with advice from a lawyer or notary.

## The hidden ledger

You may be carrying old accounts: a loan to one child, years of help from another, a promise made long ago. You may be the only person who can release them. Saying aloud that something was a gift, and is not owed, can prevent years of quarrelling after you are gone.

## Protecting what you have

Older people are often the target of scams, pressure selling and, sadly, sometimes of pressure from within their own family. Nobody, including a relative, should rush you into a decision about your money. If something feels wrong, talk to someone you trust before signing or paying anything.

Open conversations about money within the family are one of the best protections. Money that is talked about is harder to take quietly.

## The Heirloom Letter

You can write a letter about money to a grandchild or a great-grandchild, to be opened when they are grown: what you learned, what you hope for them, and what you wish you had known at their age. It will be kept with the family chronicle until the day it is opened. See [The Heirloom](../practices/heirloom.md).

## Your part in the family practices

- **[The Kitchen Table](../practices/kitchen-table.md):** you speak first when the family talks about the past.
- **[Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md):** you are the storyteller.
- **[The Family Ledger](../practices/family-ledger.md):** you may be the keeper of the chronicle, and the one who can release old debts.
- **[The Heirloom](../practices/heirloom.md):** you name what you received, what you hope the family keeps, and you may write the letter.
