# Slow Money for Adults

*For parents, carers, and adults running a household.*

You are the generation in the middle. You carry the weight of the present: the bills, the work, the decisions. You also sit between what your parents taught you about money and what your children are learning from you now, whether you mean to teach it or not.

## Your household is an economy

Your household earns, spends, saves, owes and gives as one unit, even if every account and payslip has a single name on it. Seeing it as one economy, rather than as separate individuals who happen to share a fridge, changes how you plan.

**Ask yourself:** Do the adults in this household know what the household earns in total? Does everyone know what it spends?

## The money you inherited

Before you ever earned a wage, you learned what money meant by watching your own family. Those early lessons often become beliefs that run without being noticed: that money is scarce or plentiful, dangerous or safe, something to hide or to show, to spend or to hold.

Some of these beliefs serve you well. Others may be a parent's anxiety or avoidance, carried on without being chosen.

**Ask yourself:**

- What did your parents never say about money, but clearly believed?
- Which of their habits do you repeat? Which did you swear you never would?
- When money goes wrong, what is your first feeling: fear, shame, anger, or calm?

## What your children are learning

Your children are watching you handle money. They notice the mood when a bill arrives, the silence or the argument, whether things are bought on impulse or after thought. This is their financial education, and it is more powerful than anything they are taught at school.

You don't need to hide worries from them. You can let them see money handled calmly and openly, in terms they can understand, including when it is tight.

## Talking about money as a couple

Where two adults run a household, they often bring two different inherited economies to it. Many disagreements about money are really disagreements between two families' habits.

**Try this:** each partner answers the Money Memories questions about their own childhood, then compares. Where do your families differ? Which habits will your household keep?

## Seeing both sides of the ledger

Most households track their spending better than their earning. The weekly shop is visible; the weekly income often isn't, even between partners. [The Combined Wage](../practices/combined-wage.md) helps a household see what comes in as clearly as what goes out.

## Debt and the future

Debt borrows from your future household to pay for the present one. Some debt, such as a home or a tool for work, can be worth it. Much of it is not. Before borrowing, it helps to ask not only whether you can afford the payments, but what the debt will teach your children about how a household should live.

## Planning for the long view

The middle generation is squeezed from both sides: children who need more each year, and parents who may need support. It is easy to think only as far as the next payday. Slow Money asks you to plan in decades as well: what the household will need in ten or twenty years, and what it will pass on.

## Your part in the family practices

- **[The Kitchen Table](../practices/kitchen-table.md):** you will often be the one who calls it. Make sure the youngest and oldest speak before you do.
- **[Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md):** you can be both interviewer and storyteller: ask your parents, and answer your children.
- **[The Combined Wage](../practices/combined-wage.md):** the household's income is largely yours. How openly you share it sets the tone for the family.
- **[The Family Ledger](../practices/family-ledger.md):** you are likely to hold much of the hidden ledger, in both directions.
- **[The Heirloom](../practices/heirloom.md):** you choose what your generation ends, and what it carries on.
