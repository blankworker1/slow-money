# Slow Money for Children

*For children aged about 6 to 11. Read this together with an older member of your family.*

## Money is part of your family

Your family is a team. The grown-ups in your family work, and some of what they earn pays for the things everyone shares: your home, your food, your clothes, the light and the heat.

You are part of the team too. You help by laying the table, looking after a younger brother or sister, or carrying the shopping. That help is not paid for, but it matters. A family runs on money and on help given freely.

## Where money comes from

Money does not come from the cash machine or the card. Those are only the places where money is kept and collected. Money comes from work.

When someone works, they give their time and skill, and they are paid. Then they can swap that money for the things the family needs.

**Try this:** ask a grown-up in your family what their work is, and what they do all day. How many hours do they work each week?

## Earning and receiving

There are two ways to get money:

- **Earning** is getting money for doing something, like a job or a task.
- **Receiving** is being given money as a gift, like a birthday present or pocket money.

Both are good. But money you earn teaches you something special: how much time and effort it takes to get it.

**Try this:** next time you want something, ask how many hours of work it would take to pay for it.

## Needs and wants

- A **need** is something we cannot do without: food, a home, warm clothes.
- A **want** is something we would like to have: a new toy, sweets, a game.

A family pays for its needs first. Wants come after, if there is money left.

**Try this:** at the supermarket, help sort the shopping into needs and wants. Is it always easy to tell?

## Waiting

One of the most important things you can learn about money is how to wait.

If you spend your money as soon as you get it, you can only ever buy small things. If you wait and save, you can buy something bigger later. Waiting is hard, but it gets easier with practice.

**Try this:** keep a jar for saving. Put in some of your pocket money each week. Draw a picture of what you are saving for and stick it on the jar.

## Prices change

The price of things changes over time. When your grandparents were children, a loaf of bread or an ice cream cost much less than it does today. But they earned less too.

**Try this:** ask a grandparent what an ice cream cost when they were your age. Then find out what it costs today.

## Your part in the family practices

- **[The Kitchen Table](../practices/kitchen-table.md):** you can ask any question you like about money. You may also be the first to answer when the family talks about its hopes.
- **[Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md):** you can interview a grandparent. Choose three questions, ask them, and listen carefully.
- **[The Combined Wage](../practices/combined-wage.md):** you can help share out the ten tokens to show where the family's money comes from and where it goes.
- **[The Heirloom](../practices/heirloom.md):** someone in your family may write you a letter about money, to open when you are grown up.

## For the grown-up reading with the child

Children learn more about money from watching you than from anything you tell them. They notice whether money is talked about calmly or with worry, and whether it is spent at once or saved. Letting them see how the family's money works, in simple terms, is one of the best things you can give them.
