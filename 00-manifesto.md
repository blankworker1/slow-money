# Slow Money — A Manifesto

## Money is learned at home

Before anyone opens a bank account, they already know what money means. They learned it at the kitchen table, by watching the people who fed them. Slow Money begins there.

We are not born as individuals with wallets. We are born into households that earn, spend, save, owe and give together. Named or not, every family is an economy, and how it is run shapes everything else: the arguments and the silences, the choice of school and work, who stays and who leaves.

Slow Money is a philosophy for making that first economy visible, and for running it with the long view.

## Oikos: the first economy

The word *economy* comes from the Greek *oikonomia*: the management of the household. Economics began as the art of running a home, long before it meant markets, nations or central banks.

For most of history the family was a unit of production, not only of consumption. Land, trades, tools, debts and reputations were held together and handed on. The farm, the workshop and the shop were family enterprises, and children learned the economy by working inside it.

Wage labour and the individual bank account are recent. They did not end the household economy. They only stopped us from naming it.

## Born into an economic unit

Nobody chooses their first economy. We arrive inside one, and it runs a model whether or not anyone explains it.

Children learn that model by watching, not by being taught. They learn whether money is spoken about or hidden. Whether it brings fear, shame, pride or calm. Whether it is spent the day it arrives or kept for a later that may never come. Whether debt is ordinary or a disgrace.

These lessons outlast any school course. A person can pass every financial literacy test and still be living by a grandparent's anxiety or a parent's avoidance. Slow Money starts by naming what was inherited, so it can be kept, changed or passed on by choice rather than by habit.

## The weekly shop and the missing weekly wage

Every family knows the weekly shop. It is planned together, carried in together and eaten together. Consumption is openly a family affair.

Few families know their combined weekly earnings. Fewer know their combined annual earnings. Earning is treated as private and individual, even when everything it pays for is shared.

The same split runs through modern life:

- The payslip has one name on it; the rent or mortgage feeds everyone.
- The pension belongs to one person; old age is lived inside a family.
- The credit score is individual; the debt is often carried by the household.
- Financial education is taught to pupils one by one; money habits are formed at home.
- The family is counted as one unit mainly when it asks for help, in means tests and benefit forms, rather than when it plans.

We count what we consume together and hide what we produce together. Slow Money asks families to see both sides of their own ledger: what comes in, not just what goes out.

## The family ledger, or not

Should a family keep accounts of itself? Slow Money does not answer yes or no. It asks which things belong in a ledger and which do not.

Inside most families, much of life runs like a shared table: food, care, time and help are given without anyone keeping score. Here, keeping score would itself be a breach of trust. This part of family life should stay unrecorded.

But every family also carries a hidden ledger. Who helped whom. Who was lent money and never repaid it. Who got the house. These unspoken accounts are rarely written down, and they are what break families apart at inheritance.

A slow family ledger is not a spreadsheet of each other. It records decisions and their reasons: why the land was kept, why the loan was made, what the family chose to value. It is closer to a chronicle than to bookkeeping, a memory of how the family thought about money, handed down alongside whatever money there is.

## The long view

Fast money thinks in paydays. Slow money thinks in generations.

What a family truly passes on is not a sum but an orientation to time: the habit of planning in decades, or the habit of spending the future to cover the present. Wealth can be lost in a single generation. A long view, once learned, can outlast it.

The long view is shared across ages. Grandparents remember what money did to their savings, and what they learned to trust. Parents carry the weight of the present. Children hold the horizon. A family that talks across these three generations holds a wider view of money than any one member could alone.

Slow Money treats financial mindset as an heirloom: something made carefully, kept in use and handed on on purpose.

## Good, clean and fair money

Slow Food asks that food be good, clean and fair. Slow Money asks the same of the money a family lives by.

- **Good:** money that serves family life rather than ruling it. It pays for what the family values, not only for what it is told to want.
- **Clean:** money free of hidden extraction: interest terms nobody read, fees nobody noticed, savings quietly eroded by inflation.
- **Fair:** money that is honest across generations. Nobody should inherit a ledger they did not know existed, or a debt they had no part in choosing.

## Three strands, one root

Slow Money already grows in more than one direction.

- **Where money goes.** Woody Tasch's Slow Money movement asks us to invest patiently in local food and the places we live.
- **What money is.** Self-Mint and the Coin-tainer ask whether money can be something we make, verify and hold ourselves.
- **Where money is learned.** The family strand asks how each of us first came to understand money, and what we pass on.

The third strand is the root of the other two. Before anyone invests or mints, they have been raised inside a household economy.

Slow Money begins at the kitchen table. It asks families to talk about money together, across the generations, and to decide on purpose what they will carry forward.
