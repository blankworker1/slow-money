# Money Memories

Money Memories is an interview. A younger member of the family asks an older member what money meant in their life, and listens. It is the oral history of the family's economy, told by the people who lived it.

Most of what a family knows about money is never written down. It lives in the memories of its oldest members: what things cost, what went wrong, what they learned to trust. When those memories are lost, the family loses its longest view. This practice collects them while there is still time.

## Who takes part

- **The storyteller:** a grandparent, great-aunt, older neighbour or any elder willing to talk. They decide what they share.
- **The interviewer:** a child, teenager or younger adult. The interview works best when there is a real gap of years between the two.

A parent can help a young child ask the questions, but the child should do the asking.

## What you need

- A quiet hour, ideally somewhere familiar to the storyteller.
- Something to take notes with. A recording is better still, but only if the storyteller agrees.
- Old photographs, letters, receipts, coins or banknotes, if any survive. Objects bring memories back.

## Before you begin

Ask the storyteller's permission, and tell them how the interview will be kept: notes, a recording, or nothing at all. Agree that they can skip any question and stop at any time, and that nothing will be shared beyond the family without their permission.

## The questions

Choose a handful. A good interview often follows one question for most of the hour. Young children can ask three questions and be done.

### Growing up

- What did your parents do for work?
- Did you get pocket money? What did you spend it on?
- What did your family buy, and what did it make or grow itself?
- Did people swap things or help each other instead of paying?

### First work

- What was your first job? How were you paid, and how often?
- What did you do with your first wages?
- Did you give some of your earnings to your family?

### Prices

- How much did a loaf of bread cost when you were young? A coffee? A bus ticket?
- What was the most expensive thing your family ever bought?
- What did a house cost, compared to a year's wages?

### Hard times

- Was there a time when money was very short? What did your family do?
- Did you live through a war, a bank failure, or a time when prices rose very fast?
- Do you remember when the currency changed? What happened to people's savings?

### Trust

- Where did your family keep its savings: at home, in a bank, in the post office, in gold, in land?
- Who did people trust with money? Who did they not trust?
- Did anyone in the family ever lose money? How?

### Debt

- Did your family ever borrow? Who from?
- Was debt thought of as normal, or as something shameful?

### Looking back

- What is the best thing you ever did with money?
- What do you wish you had known about money when you were my age?
- What should this family never forget?

## Tips for interviewers

- **Listen more than you ask.** The best question is often "Tell me more about that."
- **Don't correct or argue.** This is their story, as they remember it.
- **Follow the story.** If a question leads somewhere unexpected, go there and leave the list behind.
- **Ask for details.** Names, places, prices and dates make a memory come alive.
- **Allow silence.** Some memories take a moment to return.

Some memories will be painful: hunger, war, emigration, a business that failed. If the storyteller becomes upset, stop, and let them decide whether to continue.

## Then and now

After the interview, the interviewer can compare a remembered price with today's. If bread cost a certain amount then, what does it cost now? How many hours of work did it take to buy it then, and how many now?

This is one of the clearest ways to understand inflation: not as a number on the news, but as a change in what a grandparent's wages could buy. Teenagers can work it out; younger children can help find today's price at the shop.

## Afterwards

- **Share it at the table.** At the next [Kitchen Table](kitchen-table.md), the interviewer tells the family one story they heard.
- **Write one line in the chronicle.** If the family keeps a chronicle (see [The Family Ledger](family-ledger.md)), note the date, the storyteller, and one lesson they asked the family to remember.
- **Keep the recording or notes safe.** With the storyteller's permission, they can be kept with the chronicle and passed down.

## Beyond the family

Money memories are also local history. With permission, a school or community group can gather stories from many families to build a picture of how a town or valley lived with money over a lifetime. These collections can be added to local versions of this series. Every story shared beyond the family needs the storyteller's clear consent, and names can be left out.
