# Slow Money — Frequently Asked Questions

## What is Slow Money?

Slow Money is a way of thinking about money that starts with the family rather than the individual. It treats the household as the first economy each of us belongs to, and financial understanding as something passed between generations, not only taught in a classroom.

It borrows its name and its values from the Slow Food movement: money that is good, clean and fair, and a preference for the long view over the quick return.

## Who is it for?

Everyone, at every age. There are materials for children, teenagers, adults and elders, and practices designed to bring them together.

A "family" here means whoever shares a household economy. That might be parents and children, a single parent, grandparents raising grandchildren, siblings sharing a home, or a chosen family. If you share a table and a budget, Slow Money applies to you.

## Is this financial advice?

No. Slow Money does not tell anyone what to buy, sell, save in or invest in. It is about understanding: how your family thinks about money, where those habits came from, and what you want to pass on. Decisions stay with you.

## Is this about Bitcoin?

Not in itself. Slow Money has three strands: where money goes, what money is, and where money is learned. The family strand, which this series covers, is about how money is learned at home, whatever kind of money a family uses.

Other strands ask deeper questions about the nature of money, including cash, gold and Bitcoin. Nobody needs to own or use any of them to take part.

## We never talk about money at home. Where do we start?

That is the most common starting point, and the reason this series exists. Start small. The Kitchen Table practice is designed as a first conversation, with no numbers required. Many families begin by simply asking the oldest member what money meant when they were young.

## Do we have to share what everyone earns?

No. The Combined Wage exercise invites families to look at what comes in as well as what goes out, the way they already look at the weekly shop together. How far to go is each family's choice. Even talking about why earnings feel private is a useful step.

## What if money is a source of conflict in our family?

Then Slow Money may help, but go gently. Much of family life runs on giving without keeping score, and that should be protected. The Family Ledger practice helps separate what belongs on the shared table from the unspoken debts that cause lasting hurt. Where conflict is serious, outside help from a mediator or counsellor may be the right first step.

## What if we don't have much money?

Slow Money is not about wealth. It is about mindset, habits and the long view, which matter at every income. Families with little money often have the most practical knowledge to pass on.

## How is this different from school financial education?

School courses teach individual pupils facts: how interest works, what inflation is, how to budget. These are useful. But habits around money are mostly formed at home, by watching, long before any lesson. Slow Money works where those habits are formed, and across the generations who form them.

## How does this relate to Woody Tasch's Slow Money movement?

They are separate but compatible. Tasch's movement, started in the United States, encourages patient investment in local food systems and places. It asks where money goes. This series asks where money is learned. Both share the Slow Food idea that the pace and direction of money shape the places we live.

## Is there a test?

Yes, one is planned. The Two Ledgers test measures financial literacy on two axes: the international standard used by central banks, and a second module on the structure and risks of the money system itself. A household version, taken together by different generations, is being considered.

## What does it cost?

Nothing. The materials are free to read and use.
