# The Combined Wage

Every family knows its weekly shop. Few know their weekly wage. The Combined Wage is a practice for looking at what comes into the household as openly as the family already looks at what goes out.

It moves in three steps, from no numbers at all to a full picture. A family can stop at any step. Each one is useful on its own.

## Why this matters

Spending is treated as a family matter: the shop is planned, carried in and eaten together. Earning is treated as private, even when everything it pays for is shared. The result is that many people, children especially, grow up knowing the price of everything in the fridge and nothing about what it took to fill it.

A family that sees both sides of its own ledger can plan together, and its children learn early that money is earned before it is spent.

## Before you begin

Hold this practice at a [Kitchen Table](kitchen-table.md), and read the ground rules aloud. The most important one here is the first: nobody has to share numbers. Anyone who earns can keep their own figure private and still take part in every step.

Bring the receipt or list from the most recent weekly shop.

## Step one: name every source

Go around the table and list everything that comes into the household. Say it aloud or write it on a large sheet of paper. No amounts.

Include:

- Wages and salaries, and who earns them.
- Pensions, benefits and allowances.
- Money from renting, selling or trading.
- Pocket money and teenagers' first earnings.

Then add what comes in without being paid for:

- Food grown in the garden or on the land.
- Childcare from grandparents.
- Repairs done by a family member instead of a tradesperson.
- Things swapped or given by neighbours.

This second list is the household's hidden wage. It is real income, even though it never appears on a payslip. Naming it does not turn it into a debt. It stays on the table, as a gift, but now the family can see it.

## Step two: see the shares

Draw a circle or a bar on the sheet. Divide it between the sources, by rough share, without any figures. Is most of the household's income from one person or several? From work or from pensions? How large is the hidden wage?

For younger children, use ten tokens, such as coins, buttons or beans, to stand for everything that comes in during a week. Ask them to place the tokens on the sources. Then take the same ten tokens and share them out again across what the household spends: home, food, travel, clothes, fun, saving. Where do most of the tokens go? Is anything left over?

## Step three: the figure

If the family chooses, add up the combined weekly income of the household, then multiply by 52 for the year. Anyone who wants to keep their share private can add theirs in writing, folded, for one person to total.

Now place the weekly shop receipt beside the weekly figure.

- What share of the week's income goes on food?
- What share goes on the home?
- What is left after the things that must be paid?

A second way to see the same picture is in hours. How many hours of work pay for the weekly shop? For the rent or mortgage? For a single treat? Hours are often easier for children to grasp than sums, and they show that everything bought costs someone's time.

## For teenagers

A teenager with a first job is part of the household's income for the first time. The Combined Wage is a good moment to talk about what that means. Will they contribute to the household, save, spend freely, or some mix? There is no right answer, but it is better decided together than assumed.

## Closing

The family writes one line in the chronicle (see [The Family Ledger](family-ledger.md)): the date, who was present, and any decision made. Record the decision, not the figures. *We agreed to review the weekly shop together each month* is a good entry. The total income is not.

Then fold away the sheet or, if the family prefers, burn it. The figures were for seeing, not for keeping.

## For groups

Schools and community groups should never ask anyone for their household's real income. Use an imaginary family instead: give the group a set of invented earners, a hidden wage and a weekly shop receipt, and let them work through all three steps. Then send the practice home.
