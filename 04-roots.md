# Roots: A Short History of the Family Economy

Slow Money is not a new idea. For most of recorded history, economics *was* family economics. This document traces some of the places where that idea was written down, practised and, eventually, forgotten, and what each one offers families today.

## Ancient Greece: the household as the economy

The word *economy* comes from the Greek *oikonomia*, made of *oikos* (household) and *nomos* (law, custom or management). In ancient Greece the *oikos* was more than a family. It was a unit of production: the house, its land, its animals, its stores, its enslaved workers and its members across generations. Managing it well was a moral skill as much as a practical one.

Around 700 BC, the poet **Hesiod** wrote *Works and Days*, advice to his brother on running a farm household: when to plough, how to store grain, why to work steadily rather than borrow from neighbours. It is among the oldest surviving pieces of household economic advice in Europe, and much of it is about time: doing things in their season, and not spending the future to cover the present.

In the 4th century BC, **Xenophon** wrote the *Oeconomicus*, a dialogue on household management. In it, a landowner named Ischomachus describes teaching his young wife to run the household: keeping order in the stores, tracking what comes in and goes out, and training the household's workers. The dialogue treats the household as a joint enterprise between husband and wife, each with their domain, though firmly within the unequal terms of its time.

**Aristotle**, in the first book of the *Politics*, drew a distinction that still matters. *Oikonomia*, household management, is about acquiring what the household needs to live well, and it has a natural limit: a household can only use so much. *Chrematistike*, the art of money-making, has no such limit when pursued for its own sake, because money itself can be piled up forever. Aristotle regarded the first as natural and the second, when detached from the household's real needs, as unnatural.

This is perhaps the oldest version of the Slow Money question: is this money serving the life of the household, or has the household started serving the money?

## Rome: the father's household and the child's purse

In Roman law, the household (*familia*) was under the authority of its eldest male, the *paterfamilias*, who legally owned its property, including anything his adult children earned. Yet Romans also developed the *peculium*: a fund a father allowed a son or an enslaved person to manage as if it were their own. It was an early and practical form of financial training within the household, a sum held in trust so that the next generation could learn to run it.

## Italian merchant families: the ledger and the chronicle

In late medieval and Renaissance Italy, merchant families kept two kinds of book.

The first was the business ledger. Italian merchants refined double-entry bookkeeping, and in 1494 the Franciscan friar **Luca Pacioli** set out the "Venetian method" in print, spreading it across Europe. The papers of **Francesco di Marco Datini**, a 14th-century merchant of Prato, survive in enormous numbers and show how closely family, trade and accounts were bound together.

The second was the family book: the *libro di famiglia*, *libro di ricordi* or *ricordanze*. Florentine and Tuscan families kept these for generations, often passing them from father to son. They mixed accounts with family history: births, marriages and deaths, dowries paid, property bought, debts owed and settled, advice to descendants, and the reasons behind major decisions.

The *ricordanze* are the closest historical ancestor of the Slow Money chronicle: a single book holding both the family's money and its memory, written for the generations still to come.

## English country houses: the household book

The great households of medieval and early modern England kept detailed records of their daily economy. Some of the earliest surviving English household accounts belong to noble women: the household roll of **Eleanor de Montfort**, Countess of Leicester, from 1265, records what her household ate and spent, day by day.

In the early 16th century, the **Northumberland Household Book** set out the rules and budgets for the Earl of Northumberland's household in minute detail: who ate what, how much was allowed for bread, ale and candles, and who was responsible for each part of the household's spending.

In later centuries, country houses were run by a hierarchy of steward, housekeeper and butler, each keeping books of account for the master or mistress. These records survive in huge numbers in county archives and are a rich source for how large households managed food, wages, fuel and stores through the year.

They also show a less comfortable side of household records. Many of these ledgers were written by servants for their employers: accounts kept as a means of control, checked and signed off from above. A ledger can build trust, or it can be a way of watching. The Family Ledger practice is built with that difference in mind.

## The Victorian household manual

By the 19th century, household management had become a subject for printed guides, most famously **Isabella Beeton's** *Book of Household Management* (1861). Though remembered for its recipes, it opens with the duties of the mistress of the house, compared to the commander of an army, and gives detailed advice on budgets, servants' wages and keeping household accounts. For middle-class families, running the home's economy was treated as serious, skilled work.

## The wage packet on the kitchen table

In working-class households across Britain in the late 19th and 20th centuries, there was a widespread practice known in many places as "tipping up." On payday, the husband, and often the working sons and daughters too, brought home their wage packets and handed them over, often unopened, to the wife and mother. She managed the household's money for the week: rent, food, coal, clothes, the doctor, and the burial club. The earners were given back a small allowance, their "spends" or pocket money.

In 1913 **Maud Pember Reeves** published *Round About a Pound a Week*, a study of working families in Lambeth in London. It showed, week by week, how skilfully mothers stretched a small and uncertain wage across a household's needs, and how little margin they had when anything went wrong. Later, **Robert Roberts** described the same world from the inside in *The Classic Slum* (1971), his account of growing up in Salford.

This is the clearest historical example of a combined wage. Every earner's pay came to one table, one person could see the whole of what came in, and the household was run as a single economy. It was also, often, a heavy burden: the mother carried the family's financial worry alone, sometimes went without food so others could eat, and had little say over how much her husband chose to keep back. Where husbands kept back most of their wage, the family suffered.

The practice declined through the 20th century as wages were paid into individual bank accounts, household incomes rose, and earning became private. The wage packet stopped arriving on the kitchen table, and with it went much of the family's shared view of what came in.

## Family economies elsewhere

The same patterns appear wherever people have lived by the household.

- **Italian sharecropping:** under the *mezzadria*, the system of sharecropping that shaped much of rural central Italy until the mid-20th century, the contract was made with the family as a whole, not with an individual. The whole household's labour worked the farm, and the harvest was shared with the landowner.
- **Welsh inheritance:** under the medieval Welsh laws associated with Hywel Dda, land was traditionally divided among sons (*cyfran*) rather than passing to the eldest alone, so inheritance was a question for the whole family, not for one heir.
- **Dowries:** across Mediterranean Europe, including Sardinia, a daughter's dowry and trousseau were often assembled over many years by the whole family, a slow, multi-generational form of saving recorded and argued over for decades.

## What history teaches Slow Money

History offers no golden age. Most historical households were unequal, often harshly so, and much of their record-keeping served those in charge. But it does show that several things Slow Money proposes are not inventions. They are recoveries.

- **Aristotle's distinction** between managing a household and money-making for its own sake is the root of Slow Money's *good* money.
- **The Italian ricordanze** show that families have kept a chronicle of money and memory together, for generations.
- **The wage packet on the kitchen table** shows that families have seen their combined wage before, and that the view was lost only recently.
- **The Roman peculium** shows that handing a young person a purse to manage, before they inherit, is an old way to teach.
- **The country house ledger** is a warning: a family record should build trust, not surveillance.

What Slow Money adds is the insistence that these practices be shared across the whole family and every generation, rather than resting on one head of household or one overburdened mother.
