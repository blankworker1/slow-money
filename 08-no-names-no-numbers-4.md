# No Names, No Numbers #4: Lost Property

## I. Nothing Is Lost

In the city nothing was ever lost. Things were only, for a while, somewhere else.

Every umbrella, every glove, every child's shoe carried a tag the size of a grain of rice, and every tag answered to a code. When a thing was left on a tram, the tram knew before the owner did. By the time the owner's persona had shown them the small amber line, ITEM SEPARATED, a drone had usually lifted it from the seat. The Transit Authority published the figure every quarter, and every quarter it was a little better. 99.97 percent of separated items returned to holder within 4 hours.

Holder. That was the word on the forms. Not owner. He had noticed it years ago and had not known what to do with the noticing, so he had put it on a shelf, the way he put everything.

His persona read PNTLDO-7730-R. For 31 years he had kept the Lost Property Office at the Central Interchange. When he started it had filled 3 floors. Now it was one room down a corridor past the toilets, with a counter, a hatch, and shelves that went back further than the light.

What came to him now was only what the drones could not return: things without tags. Things older than the Tag Act, or made by hand, or bought from someone selling off a blanket on the pavement. A knitted hat. A walking stick with a dog's head carved into the grip. A tin of keys that opened nothing anyone remembered. A single glove, very small, in red wool.

The system logged these as untagged residue. It gave each one a reference and a declared value of nil, and after 30 days, if no holder had presented a code, it sent them for reclamation. That meant the furnace under the depot.

He had a habit he had never told anyone about. When someone came to the hatch for an untagged thing, he did not ask for their code first. He asked them to tell him about it. Not the colour; anyone could see the colour. The wear. Where the stick had been gripped so long that the dog's ears had gone smooth. Which finger of the glove the child chewed. People who had lost things could always tell you that. People who only wanted things could not.

Then he asked for the code, because the form required it, and they gave it in 0.2 seconds, and the thing went home.

## II. Residue

The crate came on a Monday from Estate Resolution. Its label was printed in the soft grey type the Authority kept for the dead.

*DECEASED CITIZEN HRTMNO-1931-L. TAGGED ASSETS REASSIGNED. BALANCE REABSORBED. CODE RETIRED. UNTAGGED RESIDUE: 1 ITEM. FORWARD TO LOST PROPERTY FOR STANDARD PERIOD.*

Inside, packed in grey foam, was a wooden box, dark with handling.

He opened it on the counter. A deck of cards, soft at the edges, of a pattern he had not seen since his grandmother's kitchen: cream faces, red and black pips, and kings and queens and knaves in stiff bright robes. A die, carved, not printed, with pictures burned into its faces where the spots should be: a crown, a star, a key, a candle, a boat, a house. And a coin.

He turned the coin under the lamp. Gold, or something like it, worn smooth. On one face, a letter B crossed by upright lines. On the other, a fine pattern of traces and dots, like a city seen from very high up.

Out of habit, he counted the cards. 52, and a joker. Complete. He weighed the coin: 8.4 grams. He measured the box. He wrote it all in the log, because that was what the log was for, gave it reference LP-44817 and a declared value of nil, and put it on the shelf nearest the hatch, where the light was best.

That evening on the tram home he found himself thinking not about the box but about the label. *Code retired.* All that a man had been, and this was what remained of him in the city: a reassignment, a reabsorption, and a box with nothing in it that anyone could put a price to.

## III. Visitors

The first was a broad woman with flour in the creases of her knuckles. She came on the Wednesday at 10:14, and when he called her code from the queue screen there was a small space, the length of a breath, before she looked up.

He would think about that breath later.

"There's a box," she said. "Wooden. From an old man's flat."

He did not tell her whether there was or not. "Tell me about it."

She told him about the cards. She didn't just say there were cards. She said their edges had gone soft as cloth, that one of the knaves had a name in faded letters by his feet, and that the queen's flower had been rubbed almost away, always by the same thumb, because whoever dealt had always held the deck the same way.

He brought the box down. She did not reach for it. She laid her hand flat on the lid, the way you lay a hand on someone's shoulder, and left it there.

"Is it yours?" he asked.

"No."

"Then whose is it?"

She thought about the question with real care. "It was his," she said. "And before that, someone else's. It was given to him with the box."

"I can only release it to a holder."

"Yes," she said, as if that were sad but not surprising. She took her hand away, and went.

The next day a girl came with ink on her fingers that would not wash off. She told him about the die: that the crown always lay opposite the house, the star opposite the boat, the key opposite the candle, as if whoever made it had wanted every tale to have its other side. He had not noticed. He checked after she had gone. She was right.

Then a young man came who described nothing at all. He asked only to see it. Then he looked at it, the box and each thing in it, for so long and with such attention that the queue behind him began to mutter. When he had finished he thanked the clerk and left.

The fourth was a woman in a grey coat, and she told him about the coin. "The mark is fortune," she said, "and the maze is trouble."

"What was it?" he asked, before he could stop himself. "Before."

"Nobody knows. He didn't know either. He used to say it must have meant something once."

Every one of them had paused before answering their code. The same breath of nothing. In 31 years he had called many thousands of codes across that counter, and he had never heard so many silences in a single week.

None of them said it was theirs.

## IV. The Form

On the Friday the notice arrived in amber.

*LOST PROPERTY OFFICE, CENTRAL INTERCHANGE: CLOSURE AT END OF QUARTER. RESIDUAL INVENTORY TO RECLAMATION. STAFF REDEPLOYMENT INTERVIEW THURSDAY 09:00.*

He read it, then looked up the intake date on LP-44817 and counted forward on his fingers, which he had not done since he was a boy. Its 30 days would end 2 days after the office closed. It made no difference. Reclamation was reclamation.

The woman with flour on her knuckles came back the following week. She asked for nothing. She only stood at the hatch, as if she had come to visit.

"Listen," he said, and he lowered his voice, although there was no one in the queue. "Give me a code. Anyone's. Yours. It's only a form." He turned the screen so she could see it. *Field 1: claimant code. Field 2: item reference. Field 3: declared value.* "I fill in the rest. It's yours in a minute."

"If I put my code on it," she said, "it becomes mine."

"Isn't that what you want?"

"If it's mine, it can be lost." She looked past him, at the shelves going back into the dark: the hat, the stick with the dog's head, the tin of keys, the small red glove. "Everything back there belonged to somebody. That's why it's here."

He had no answer to that.

She took something from her coat and laid it on the counter: a small loaf, wrapped in paper. It had no tag. His persona did not register it at all.

"That isn't for the box," she said. "That's for you. Bring it back to me unopened, and I'll take you somewhere."

## V. Reclamation

He thought about it for 3 nights.

He thought about the 31 years. Everything that had crossed his counter had been somebody's, and every time he had known exactly whose: the code, the reference, the date and time of return. He had been very good at it. And in all that time he had never known anything about any of the people beyond their codes, and he had never once thought that strange, until a woman put her hand on the lid of a box as if it were a shoulder.

Holder, not owner. The thing was held by the code. And the code was held by … He had never finished that thought, either. He finished it now, lying in the dark, and did not sleep afterwards.

On the morning of the last day he opened the record for LP-44817, put the cursor in Field 1, and typed his own code. Then he sat looking at it for a long while before he pressed enter, because he understood what he was doing. Somewhere, for as long as there was a somewhere, the city would believe he owned a wooden box. If anyone ever asked for it, he would have to answer for it.

The reclamation van came at 16:00. He loaded the residue himself: the hat, the stick, the tin of keys, the red glove nobody had come for. The driver scanned each reference. At the end he looked up.

"There's a box on the manifest."

"Released," he said. "To holder."

The driver's persona checked his. Green. The van pulled away.

He switched off the lights one row at a time, from the back of the room to the front, until only the hatch was lit. His persona offered him a small gold line: *31 YEARS SERVICE. THANK YOU, CITIZEN.* He carried the box out under one arm, with the loaf in his coat pocket. The loaf was the only thing he had that weighed more than its record.

## VI. The Basket

When the street lights came on, the woman with flour on her knuckles was waiting outside the bakery.

He gave her the loaf. She weighed it in her hand, and nodded, and walked him through streets he had crossed all his life without seeing them. Past the fountain with no water in it. Left at the wall with the pale shape of a horse. Down the lane that smelled of the soap works, to a house where a fig tree had grown up through the yard and split the wall.

In the hallway there was a deep wicker basket. She took off her wristband and laid it in. Then she found the edge of her persona with a fingernail, and lifted it away.

He did the same, with the handset, the card, the sleeve, the band. His hands were steadier than he expected. When the persona came away, the air of the hallway touched his face, and for a moment he could not see anything at all, because there was nothing written on it.

He held out the box. "Does this go in as well?"

She looked at it, and then at him, almost tenderly. "Was it ever anyone's?"

He carried it in.

The kitchen was warm and full of faces, and they turned to look at him slowly. He knew some of them without knowing how: the girl with the ink, the young man who had looked, the woman in the grey coat. The table was laid a little wider than the company, as if it were expecting someone. At its head there was a chair with no one in it.

Nobody told him where to put the box. He set it down in front of the empty chair, and the kitchen went quiet.

Then the girl with the ink on her fingers began, in a level voice, as if telling a story everyone already knew:

*Anyone may leave, and take nothing, because there is nothing to take.*

She did not go on to the next. She let that one stand.

Nobody spoke the old man's name, because there was no name to speak. Instead they spoke of him. The one with hands like roots. The one who broke the bread. The one who said *no, thank you* more kindly than anyone. The one who sat up with whoever was ill. Somebody laughed, remembering something, and somebody else finished the story for them. Nobody sat in the chair. When the bread came, it was broken where it lay, by the nearest hands.

He was a novice, and he wanted to give them something, so he said what he had. "I kept that office for thirty-one years."

"No, thank you," said the woman in the grey coat, gently.

He felt his face burn. He thought about it. "A long time," he said.

Around the table, people nodded.

Later, when the soup had gone round, he tried again, more carefully. "In all that time," he said, "I sent every single thing back to the right code. I never once gave anything back to a person."

Nobody said *no, thank you* to that.

When the evening was coming to its end, the woman with flour on her knuckles opened the box. She took a pinch from the deck by feel and slid the Joker into it, and the girl rolled the die, and it came down on the key. Then the woman in the grey coat turned the coin over in her fingers, the mark and then the maze, the thing nobody could remember the meaning of, and put it back in his hand.

"Toss it," she said.

He had held it under a lamp and weighed it to a tenth of a gram. He had never once wondered what it was for. He tossed it, and it rang on the table, and came down on the mark.

So the tale would begin with fortune, and he was the one to begin it.

## VII. Holder

Somewhere in the city, on a server he would never see, a record sat in a closed file. *LP-44817. Box, wood, with contents. Declared value nil. Released to holder: PNTLDO-7730-R.* On the city's books, the box was his.

At the table it lived where it had always lived: in front of the empty chair, among the cups. It belonged to no one there. It went home with no one. When the bread ran out, the laid cards were wrapped in their cloth and the box was put back under the bench, to wait for the next evening the rain kept people late.

In the hallway, as he lifted his persona back onto his face, he felt for the first time the slight weight of it. Then he heard someone speak of him to someone else, quietly, as people do once a thing has been settled.

"That's the one who brought the box home."

And nothing, he found, had been lost at all.
