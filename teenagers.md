# Slow Money for Teenagers

*For ages about 12 to 17.*

You are at the point where money stops being something that happens around you and starts being something you handle yourself. You may already have a first job, a bank card or a phone full of ways to pay. This is also the age when the habits you have been absorbing at home start to show.

This track covers what is worth understanding now, before the stakes get higher.

## You grew up inside an economy

You have spent your whole life inside your family's economy: its income, its spending, its worries and its habits. Some of your attitudes to money were learned there, without anyone teaching them to you.

**Ask yourself:** When money comes up at home, is the mood calm, tense or silent? Do you think that has shaped how you feel about money?

## Your first earnings

A first job changes your place in the household. For the first time, you are part of what comes in, not only what goes out.

It is worth deciding, with your family, what happens to that money. Will some go to the household? Will you save part of it? Will you spend it freely? There is no single right answer, but it is better decided together than assumed.

**Worth knowing:** your pay may arrive with deductions for tax or contributions. Learn to read a payslip, and ask someone to explain anything you don't understand.

## Interest works both ways

When you save money, you may be paid interest. When you borrow, you pay it. And interest compounds: you earn or pay interest on the interest, not just the original sum.

Over long periods this makes a huge difference. Small savings left for decades can grow a lot. Small debts left unpaid can grow just as fast, against you.

**Try this:** find out the interest rate on a credit card or a "buy now, pay later" offer. How much would a purchase really cost if you paid it back slowly?

## Inflation

Over time, most prices rise. That means money kept in a drawer buys a little less each year. Inflation is one reason your grandparents remember such different prices.

**Try this:** ask a grandparent what something cost when they were your age, then compare it to today's price and to what they earned then. See [Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md).

## Debt

Borrowing is not always bad. It can pay for an education, a home or a tool that earns more than it costs. But debt takes money from your future self and gives it to your present self, and your future self has to pay it back with interest.

Before borrowing, ask: *Will this still be worth it when I have finished paying for it?*

## Whose promise is it?

Money comes in different forms. Cash in your hand is one. The number in a bank account is another: it is a promise from the bank that it owes you that money. A balance in an app is a promise from the company behind the app. People have also kept their savings in gold, land and, more recently, Bitcoin.

Each form carries different risks. Some depend on trusting a bank or a company to keep its promise. Others depend on keeping something safe yourself. Slow Money does not tell you which to use. It asks you to understand what you are holding, and who, if anyone, you are trusting.

**Ask yourself:** For each place you keep money, who would you have to trust for it to still be there next year?

## Wanting and being sold to

You are the target of more advertising than any generation before you, much of it designed to make you spend now rather than later. Noticing when you are being sold to is a financial skill.

**Try this:** for one week, notice every time something on your phone tries to get you to buy. How many times was it?

## The long view

The most valuable money habit is the ability to wait: to put off something small now for something larger later. Economists call this time preference. People who learn it young carry it for the rest of their lives.

## Your part in the family practices

- **[The Kitchen Table](../practices/kitchen-table.md):** you can call one yourself. You don't need to wait for an adult.
- **[Money Memories](../practices/money-memories.md):** you are the ideal interviewer. Ask an elder the questions nobody else has thought to ask.
- **[The Combined Wage](../practices/combined-wage.md):** if you earn, you are now part of the household's income. You can also help work out what the weekly shop costs in hours of work.
- **[The Family Ledger](../practices/family-ledger.md):** you can take part in deciding what the family records, and what it leaves as a gift.
- **[The Heirloom](../practices/heirloom.md):** you can name one habit you want to keep, and one you want to end with your generation.
